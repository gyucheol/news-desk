# Traditional (non-AI) DSP for cleaning up and polishing spoken-voice recordings, and the loudness standards used to finish them

Research date: 2026-10-22. Labels used below: **[DOC]** = official standard, primary paper or official software/platform documentation; **[RoT]** = rule of thumb from practitioner/vendor articles (not a standard); **[INF]** = my own inference; **(via search summary)** = the claim was seen only in a search-engine summary of that page, not in a full-text fetch, so wording/attribution should be re-checked before quoting. Product docs (Audacity, FFmpeg, iZotope RX) are used here only for what they say about *methods*, not as feature lists. ML/"AI" tools (e.g., FFmpeg `arnndn`, iZotope Dialogue De-reverb/Dialogue Isolate) are out of scope and mentioned only to mark the boundary.

## 1. Noise reduction: spectral subtraction, Wiener filtering, MMSE-STSA / log-MMSE, noise-profile learning and spectral gating; causes of "musical noise"/"watery" artifacts and how tools mitigate them

### Takeaway
All classic broadband noise reducers work frame-by-frame in the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) domain: estimate the noise spectrum (from a noise-only "profile", by tracking, or by minimum statistics), compute a gain for each frequency bin from the estimated signal-to-noise ratio, multiply, and resynthesize. They differ mainly in the gain rule (Boll's subtraction, Wiener, Ephraim–Malah MMSE) and in how the gain is smoothed and floored. Their signature artifact, "musical noise" (random short tones, "tinkly bells"), comes from random frame-to-frame fluctuations of the noisy spectrum surviving a hard gain rule; tools reduce it by smoothing gains over time/frequency, limiting the maximum attenuation (a "floor"), or using Ephraim–Malah's smoothed "decision-directed" a priori SNR. All of them work well only when the noise is steady and much quieter than the voice.

### Cited Findings

#### (a) What problem it fixes / when it works
- [DOC] Audacity's Noise Reduction "can reduce constant background sounds such as hum, whistle, whine, buzz, and 'hiss', such as tape hiss, fan noise or FM/webcast carrier noise. It is not suitable for individual clicks and pops, or irregular background noise such as from traffic or an audience." — [Audacity Manual: Noise Reduction](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/noise_reduction.html)
- [DOC] Audacity warns satisfactory removal may be impossible "when the noise is very loud, when the noise is variable, when the music or speech is not much louder than the noise or when the noise frequencies are very similar to those of the music or speech." — [Audacity Manual: Noise Reduction](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/noise_reduction.html)
- [DOC] iZotope's Spectral De-noise is described as removing "stationary or slowly changing tonal noise and broadband hiss by learning a profile of the offending noise and then subtracting it from the signal" (tape hiss, HVAC, outdoor environments, line noise, ground loops, camera motors, fans, wind, complex buzz). — [iZotope RX 9 manual: Spectral De-noise](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/spectral-de-noise/index.html)
- [DOC] In typical office and real-world environments the background noise is dominated by low frequencies; low-quality hardware can also leak distortion that shows up as peaks at higher frequencies. — [Aalto "Introduction to Speech Processing": Noise attenuation](https://speechprocessingbook.aalto.fi/enhancement/noise-attenuation)

#### (b) How it works — the shared STFT framework and noise estimation
- [DOC] The STFT spectrum is a good domain for noise attenuation because spectral components can "reasonably safely" be assumed uncorrelated, so each frequency bin can be treated with a simple scalar gain (O(N) rather than matrix operations). — [Aalto: Noise attenuation](https://speechprocessingbook.aalto.fi/enhancement/noise-attenuation)
- [DOC] Noise statistics must first be estimated from noise-only sections, e.g., found with a voice activity detector (VAD); this assumes the noise is stationary (noise in non-speech parts resembles noise under speech), and "VADs are accurate only at low noise levels." — [Aalto: Noise attenuation](https://speechprocessingbook.aalto.fi/enhancement/noise-attenuation)
- [DOC] Alternative: the minimum-statistics estimate (Martin, 2001) takes the minimum energy of each frequency component over time; it is biased low but preserves spectral shape, the bias can be corrected with a constant, and it avoids a VAD threshold. — [Aalto: Noise attenuation](https://speechprocessingbook.aalto.fi/enhancement/noise-attenuation)

#### (b) Spectral subtraction (Boll 1979) and Berouti's over-subtraction/floor (1979)
- [DOC] Bibliographic: S. F. Boll, "Suppression of acoustic noise in speech using spectral subtraction," IEEE Trans. ASSP, 1979, DOI 10.1109/TASSP.1979.1163209 (Semantic Scholar counts ~5,176 citations). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/TASSP.1979.1163209); [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1109/TASSP.1979.1163209?fields=title,year,venue,citationCount)
- [DOC] Mechanism: subtract the estimated noise energy from the energy of each noisy STFT bin; because the noise estimate is imperfect (and speech and noise are not exactly uncorrelated) the result can go negative, so it is thresholded at zero; the per-bin gain is sqrt(enhanced energy / observed energy). — [Aalto: Noise attenuation](https://speechprocessingbook.aalto.fi/enhancement/noise-attenuation)
- [DOC] Aalto's assessment: spectral subtraction usually improves quality "but it also leaves room for improvement. In particular, usually, we hear musical noise in the higher frequencies"; it is "a powerful method when taking into account how simple it is." — [Aalto: Noise attenuation](https://speechprocessingbook.aalto.fi/enhancement/noise-attenuation)
- [DOC] Bibliographic: Berouti et al., "Enhancement of speech corrupted by acoustic noise," ICASSP 1979, DOI 10.1109/ICASSP.1979.1170788. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICASSP.1979.1170788); [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1109/ICASSP.1979.1170788?fields=title,year,venue,citationCount)
- Berouti's remedies (via search summary): over-subtract the noise estimate by a factor α ≥ 1 (removes the isolated "islands" of spectral energy) and apply a spectral floor β instead of zeroing (raises the valleys between peaks); too much over-subtraction distorts speech, too little leaves musical noise. — [Complete Communications Engineering (vocal.com): Spectral subtraction](https://vocal.com/noise-reduction/spectral-subtraction/) (via search summary)

#### (b) Wiener filtering
- [DOC] Wiener filtering is the linear filter that minimizes the expected error energy (MMSE); it "provides an analytical expression for an optimal solution in noise attenuation" with "very well-documented properties and performance guarantees." — [Aalto: Noise attenuation](https://speechprocessingbook.aalto.fi/enhancement/noise-attenuation)
- [DOC] Both spectral subtraction and Wiener filtering really use only the *ratio* of speech to noise energy (the SNR) to set each bin's gain, so better speech/noise models translate into better SNR estimates and masks. — [Aalto: Noise attenuation](https://speechprocessingbook.aalto.fi/enhancement/noise-attenuation)
- [DOC] In Aalto's demonstrations, the MMSE estimate "is not drastically different from the spectral subtraction algorithm," but MMSE "is more robust and therefore typically has better quality at low SNR," where energy-threshold gating "will surely fail." — [Aalto: Noise attenuation](https://speechprocessingbook.aalto.fi/enhancement/noise-attenuation)

#### (b) Ephraim–Malah MMSE-STSA (1984) and log-MMSE (1985)
- [DOC] Bibliographic: Y. Ephraim & D. Malah, "Speech enhancement using a minimum mean-square error short-time spectral amplitude estimator," IEEE Trans. ASSP, DOI 10.1109/TASSP.1984.1164453; and "Speech enhancement using a minimum mean-square error log-spectral amplitude estimator," DOI 10.1109/TASSP.1985.1164550 (Semantic Scholar's metadata lists the latter's year as 1984 while its DOI encodes 1985 — a metadata discrepancy). — [DOI 1984](https://doi.org/10.1109/TASSP.1984.1164453); [DOI 1985](https://doi.org/10.1109/TASSP.1985.1164550); [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1109/TASSP.1985.1164550?fields=title,year,venue,citationCount)
- [DOC] Cappé (1994) explains why the Ephraim–Malah suppression rule (EMSR) avoids musical noise: it has been "used recently for the restoration of degraded audio recordings because it is free of the frequently encountered 'musical noise' artifact," and "this artifact is actually eliminated without bringing distortion to the recorded signal even if the noise is only poorly stationary." In the original papers this was only "an experimental finding." — [Cappé 1994, IEEE Trans. Speech Audio Process. (author copy)](https://www.di.ens.fr/olivier.cappe/Publications/Self-archive/ephma94.pdf); [DOI 10.1109/89.279283](https://doi.org/10.1109/89.279283)
- [DOC] Mechanism per Cappé: the "a priori SNR" carries information gathered from previous frames and is computed by a recursive "decision-directed" rule whose weighting the authors set to about 0.98; the a priori SNR is the dominant parameter (strong attenuation only when it is low). When the a posteriori (instantaneous) SNR is near or below 0 dB, the a priori SNR is "a highly smoothed version" of it over successive frames; when the a posteriori SNR is much greater than 0 dB, it simply follows it with a one-frame delay. "The smoothness of the a priori SNR helps reducing the musical noise effect." — [Cappé 1994](https://www.di.ens.fr/olivier.cappe/Publications/Self-archive/ephma94.pdf)
- Trade-offs of the decision-directed smoothing (via search summary of later papers): smoothing with factor ≈0.98 can smear speech transitions/onsets and the one-frame lag can cause transient distortion. — [EUSIPCO 2006 paper](https://new.eurasip.org/Proceedings/Eusipco/Eusipco2006/papers/1568980470.pdf); [Suhadi & Fingscheidt, Interspeech 2007](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2007/suhadi07_interspeech.pdf) (via search summary)

#### (b) Noise-profile ("noise print") learning and threshold-based spectral gating as implemented in tools
- [DOC] Audacity, step 1 "Get Noise Profile": select a region containing only noise, minimum 2048 samples (0.05 s at 44.1 kHz), "A longer Noise Profile is better"; different noise types in different places should be profiled and reduced one at a time; duplicating a short noise sample does not help because "Noise Reduction calculates the statistics of the noise." — [Audacity Manual: Noise Reduction](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/noise_reduction.html)
- [DOC] Audacity, step 2 controls: **Noise Reduction (dB)** = amount of attenuation applied to identified noise ("Use the lowest value that reduces the noise to an acceptable level"; default 6); **Sensitivity** (0–24, default 6.00) = how much of the audio is considered noise; **Frequency Smoothing (bands)** spreads the reduction into neighbouring bands, each band = sample rate/2048 = 21.53 Hz at 44.1 kHz, default 6, "settings lower than this tend to favor music and higher settings tend to favor spoken word"; **time smoothing** is always applied (attack 20 ms, release 100 ms, not configurable); **Residue** mode lets you hear what will be removed. — [Audacity Manual: Noise Reduction](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/noise_reduction.html)
- [DOC] FFmpeg `afftdn` (FFT denoiser): `noise_reduction` default 12 dB (range 0.01–97); `noise_floor` default −50 dB; `noise_type` white/vinyl/shellac/custom (15-band profile); `residual_floor` default −38 dB; `track_noise` (automatic noise-floor tracking); `adaptivity` (how fast per-bin gains adapt, default 0.5); `band_multiplier`; `sample_noise` start/stop to capture a noise profile from the input; `gain_smooth` (spatial smoothing radius of per-bin gains, "Useful to reduce random music noise artefacts"); output modes to pass only the removed noise. Examples: `afftdn=nr=10:nf=-40`; `afftdn=nr=10:nf=-80:tn=1`; profile from the first 0.4 s via `asendcmd`. — [FFmpeg Filters: afftdn](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html#afftdn)
- [DOC] Other classic (non-ML) FFmpeg denoisers: `anlmdn` "Reduce broadband noise in audio samples using Non-Local Means algorithm. Each sample is adjusted by looking for other samples with similar contexts" (patch radius default 2 ms, research radius default 6 ms); `afwtdn` reduces broadband noise "using Wavelets." By contrast `arnndn` uses "Recurrent Neural Networks" (ML, out of scope). — [FFmpeg Filters: anlmdn](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html#anlmdn); [afwtdn](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html#afwtdn); [arnndn](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html#arnndn)
- [DOC] iZotope Spectral De-noise "learns a profile of the background noise, then subtracts that noise when a signal's amplitude drops below the specified threshold"; a learned profile "remains fixed for the duration of processing" and suits constant noise, while Adaptive mode changes the profile with the incoming audio for changing noise (traffic, waves). A separate control sets "how much noise reduction will depend upon either spectral subtraction or wide band gating." — [iZotope RX 9: Spectral De-noise](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/spectral-de-noise/index.html)
- [DOC] Its four algorithms illustrate the anti-musical-noise toolbox: A "reduces musical noise artifacts by time smoothing of the signal spectrum"; B uses "adaptive 2D smoothing (both time and frequency)"; C adds "multiresolution operation for better handling of signal transients and even fewer musical noise artifacts"; D adds "high-frequency synthesis for reconstruction of signal details buried in noise." — [iZotope RX 9: Spectral De-noise](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/spectral-de-noise/index.html)
- [DOC] A whole-signal noise gate (energy threshold with hysteresis and fade-in/out) is the simplest relative; it "is a simple implementation of a voice activity detector (VAD)." — [Aalto: Noise attenuation](https://speechprocessingbook.aalto.fi/enhancement/noise-attenuation)

#### (c) Typical settings for spoken voice
- [DOC] Audacity's defaults are 6 dB reduction / sensitivity 6 / 6 smoothing bands; if artifacts remain after raising Sensitivity as far as possible, try frequency smoothing of 1–6 bands; where the signal is strong and wide-band and the noise light, smoothing 0 may be better. — [Audacity Manual: Noise Reduction](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/noise_reduction.html)
- [DOC] iZotope's worked dialogue example chose 18 dB of Spectral De-noise; the module "can do as much as 40 dB, but it can have a dire effect on voice quality—for this reason, it's mainly used in audio forensics." A gentler combination (a voice-specific denoiser pass followed by Spectral De-noise at −12 dB) gave milder artifacts. — [iZotope: Order of operations in audio repair](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/order-of-audio-repair-operations.html)
- [DOC] FFmpeg's own examples use 10–20 dB of reduction (`nr=10`, `nr=20`). — [FFmpeg Filters: afftdn](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html#afftdn)
- [DOC] The deliverable spec for Audible/ACX audiobooks requires a noise floor below −60 dB RMS, which effectively sets a noise-reduction target for narration. — [ACX Audio Submission Requirements](https://help.acx.com/s/article/acx-audio-submission-requirements)

#### (d) Position in the chain
- [DOC] Audacity: "Do any Notch Filtering or Click Removal before doing noise reduction"; "Do any compression or any other effects not mentioned above after doing noise reduction, not before"; amplify/normalize may be done before or after. — [Audacity Manual: Noise Reduction](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/noise_reduction.html)
- [DOC] iZotope: broadband noise reducers "need to work with the cleanest possible audio," so clipping, stereo issues, clicks and hum are fixed first; then steady noise, then variable noise. — [iZotope: Order of operations](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/order-of-audio-repair-operations.html)

#### (e) Side effects, artifacts, limits — musical noise and "watery/phasey" sound
- [DOC] Audacity describes artifacts as "random bursts of very short tones at random frequencies, sometimes called 'musical noise', 'bird song' or 'tinkly-bells'," visible in spectrogram view; they arise "if the noise floor is higher than was estimated, either because Sensitivity was set too low, or because the Noise Profile was not representative," and "can be even more objectionable than the original noise." — [Audacity Manual: Noise Reduction](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/noise_reduction.html)
- [DOC] Root cause (Cappé): the short-time magnitude of noise "exhibits strong fluctuations in noisy areas," so after attenuation the noise-only bands become "a succession of randomly spaced spectral peaks," i.e., "sinusoidal components with random frequencies that come and go in each short-time frame." Fixes that merely modify the rule "only reduce the musical noise"; complete elimination by "crude overestimation of the noise average spectrum" over-attenuates and "can generate audible distortions." — [Cappé 1994](https://www.di.ens.fr/olivier.cappe/Publications/Self-archive/ephma94.pdf)
- [DOC] Lebart et al. note the rectified negative values yield musical noise that is masked while speech is present "but it is clearly perceptible during periods of silence." — [Lebart, Boucher & Denbigh 2001, Acta Acustica](https://www.ee.columbia.edu/~dpwe/papers/LebBD01-ssdereverv.pdf)
- [DOC] Aalto: musical noise "is perceived when spectral components are randomly turned on and off"; the hard threshold at zero causes it; remedies are a noise floor / noise filling (add noise whenever components fall below a threshold) and soft thresholds (e.g., softplus). Tune "just above the level where musical noise is perceived since it is a non-natural distortion and therefore more annoying than an incomplete noise attenuation." — [Aalto: Noise attenuation](https://speechprocessingbook.aalto.fi/enhancement/noise-attenuation)
- [DOC] Audacity trade-offs: decreasing Sensitivity reduces loss of wanted sound but increases "tinkly bells"; decreasing the reduction amount keeps more wanted sound but leaves more noise; "Reducing noise usually results in some distortion. This is normal and there is nothing you can do about it." If recognizable bits of the voice are audible in the Residue, reduction or sensitivity is too high. — [Audacity Manual: Noise Reduction](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/noise_reduction.html)
- [DOC] iZotope observed "slightly phasey artifacts" at soft parts of the voice (ends of phrases) and a "hollow" quality at 18 dB; running two lighter passes (15 dB then 6 dB) made "the hollow phasing artifacts" worse in that example, though multiple light passes can sometimes be "less obtrusive." — [iZotope: Order of operations](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/order-of-audio-repair-operations.html)
- [DOC] Threshold trade-off: higher thresholds "reduce more noise, but also suppress low-level signal components"; lower thresholds preserve detail "but can result in noise being modulated by the signal." — [iZotope RX 9: Spectral De-noise](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/spectral-de-noise/index.html)
- [DOC] Whole-band gating of noisy speech "distorted the speech signal significantly. In particular, typically we lose plosives at the beginning of words," sounds odd switching on/off, and per-region gating still leaves "musical noise in the low and high frequencies." — [Aalto: Noise attenuation](https://speechprocessingbook.aalto.fi/enhancement/noise-attenuation)
- [DOC] Profile limits: a profile taken at one sample rate cannot be used on another; for cassettes the noise can change over the tape. — [Audacity Manual: Noise Reduction](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/noise_reduction.html)

### Inferences
- [INF] Audacity's controls describe what is usually called "spectral gating": a per-band threshold set relative to the learned noise floor (Sensitivity), a fixed maximum attenuation (Noise Reduction dB), and time/frequency smoothing of the resulting gains. The fixed maximum attenuation plays the same role as Berouti's spectral floor and FFmpeg's `residual_floor`: by never pushing a bin to zero, it trades residual noise for fewer "on/off" tones.
- [INF] "Underwater/watery/phasey/hollow" descriptions most likely refer to the same mechanism heard on voiced speech rather than in pauses: strongly time-varying per-bin gains plus heavy smoothing remove low-level harmonics and high-frequency detail and modulate what remains. iZotope's "phasey"/"hollow" wording and Aalto's "metallic and muffled" description of an over-smoothed output support this, but I found no primary source that defines "underwater" as a term.
- [INF] For speech the practical mitigation hierarchy is: (1) record quieter noise (no DSP beats SNR); (2) a representative, long profile; (3) the least attenuation that is acceptable (Audacity default 6 dB; most examples 10–18 dB); (4) gain smoothing / higher smoothing bands (Audacity says higher favors speech); (5) check the residue for voice content.
- [INF] Fixed-profile methods suit stationary hiss/HVAC; adaptive/tracking modes (FFmpeg `track_noise`, iZotope Adaptive) handle drifting noise but risk treating sustained voice as noise. This is inferred from the docs' framing and was not tested.

## 2. Hum/buzz removal (50/60 Hz + harmonics; notch/comb filters) and high-pass/low-cut filtering for rumble and plosives — typical cutoffs for voice

### Takeaway
Mains hum is periodic, so it is removed with very narrow notches at the fundamental (50 or 60 Hz) and its harmonics, effectively a comb filter, set fixed or adaptive and applied before broadband noise reduction. Rumble and plosive "thumps" sit mostly below ~100–300 Hz, where speech carries little useful energy, so a high-pass (low-cut) filter is the standard fix. The usual voice cutoff of ~80–100 Hz is a rule of thumb, not a standard; steeper or higher cutoffs remove more pops but thin the voice, and a dedicated de-plosive is recommended before the HPF.

### Cited Findings

#### Hum/buzz — (a) problem and (b) mechanism
- [DOC] "Hum is often caused by lack of proper electrical ground." iZotope De-hum "includes a series of notch filters that can be set to remove both the base frequency of the hum (usually 50 or 60 Hz) as well as any harmonics"; the plug-in handles "up to seven harmonics above its primary frequency." — [iZotope De-hum Plug-in](https://help.izotope.com/docs/rx-plug-in-pack/De-hum.html)
- [DOC] De-hum options: Learn (auto-detects the fundamental, more reliable on hum-only audio); Adaptive mode (re-estimates over time for "constantly evolving" sources) vs Manual (fixed frequency); "Free" frequency for off-speed analog tape; Filter Q (bandwidth of the notches); harmonic slope/linking (odd/even); "Output hum only" to audition what is removed. — [iZotope De-hum Plug-in](https://help.izotope.com/docs/rx-plug-in-pack/De-hum.html)
- [DOC] Filter-design trade-off: linear-phase FIR notches have "very accurate frequency response at the expense of latency and filter pre-ringing"; minimum-phase IIR notches are "only susceptible to post-ringing (which is usually less noticeable than the pre-ringing introduced by FIR filters)." — [iZotope De-hum Plug-in](https://help.izotope.com/docs/rx-plug-in-pack/De-hum.html)
- [DOC] Audacity's Notch Filter "sharply attenuates frequency-specific noise like mains hum or electrical whistle with minimal damage to the remaining audio, by cutting a 'notch' out of the frequency spectrum." Use 60 Hz for North America and 50 Hz for the UK and most other countries. — [Audacity Manual: Notch Filter](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/notch_filter.html)
- [DOC] FFmpeg offers generic `bandreject` and `equalizer` filters, which can be used for notching. Its filter documentation (fetched 2026-10-22) contains no filter described as removing hum. — [FFmpeg Filters: bandreject](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html#bandreject)

#### Hum/buzz — (c) typical settings
- [DOC] Audacity: notch the fundamental, then use Plot Spectrum to find other spikes (examples given: 180 Hz, 300 Hz) and notch each; "a Q of between 2 and 10 works well for mains hum removal" (default Q = 1); raise Q for higher harmonics "to lessen possible audio artifacts"; finish with "Noise Reduction at mild settings to remove any residual harmonics." — [Audacity Manual: Notch Filter](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/notch_filter.html)
- [DOC] The RX module version can go "up to 16 harmonics," linked or odd/even independently. — [iZotope: Order of operations](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/order-of-audio-repair-operations.html)

#### Hum/buzz — (d) position and (e) side effects
- [DOC] Before broadband noise reduction: Audacity says to use a Notch Filter for hum/whistle "before applying Noise Reduction"; iZotope ranks clicks and hum as "deeper" damage than broadband noise, so they are fixed first. — [Audacity Manual: Noise Reduction](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/noise_reduction.html); [iZotope: Order of operations](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/order-of-audio-repair-operations.html)
- [DOC] Over-processing: running De-hum where there is no hum produced "whistling resonance under the voice. The more harmonics you add, the worse it gets, until everything sounds like it's being heard through a metal tube, due to all that comb filtering… Hey, we just re-invented the flanger!" — [iZotope: Order of operations](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/order-of-audio-repair-operations.html)
- [DOC] A notch can be "notched too deeply, thus affecting the content," in which case a higher Q (narrower notch) is preferable. — [Audacity Manual: Notch Filter](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/notch_filter.html)

#### High-pass / low-cut — (a, b) problem and mechanism
- [DOC] A high-pass filter passes frequencies above the cutoff and progressively attenuates those below; the cutoff is the −3 dB point (so there is slight attenuation just above it); roll-off (dB/octave) sets the steepness, e.g., 6 dB/octave = 6 dB less per octave below cutoff; for more attenuation, run it again or use a steeper roll-off. — [Audacity Manual: High-Pass Filter](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/high_pass_filter.html)
- [DOC] Speech covers roughly 100 Hz–8 kHz (harmonics to ~12 kHz); "Maximum speech power is in the 250 and 500 Hz bands." — [JBL Professional Technical Note: Speech Intelligibility](https://proaudioencyclopedia.com/speech-intelligibility-a-jbl-professional-technical-note-technical-notes-volume-1-no-26/)
- [DOC] Plosives: a consonant in which "the vocal tract briefly blocks the flow of air"; the released pressure hitting the microphone diaphragm causes the "pop." "Plosives typically manifest between 20Hz to 300Hz, but may sometimes reach as high as 500Hz." Pop filters (studio) or wind filters (location) are the recording-time prevention. — [iZotope RX 9: De-plosive](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/de-plosive/index.html)

#### High-pass / low-cut — (c) typical cutoffs (rules of thumb)
- [RoT] An existing plosive's "energy is almost entirely below about 100 Hz," and a high-pass around 80–100 Hz removes most of the thump with little loss of voice content. — [IRPR Sound: Plosive pops](https://sounddesign.irpr.agency/solutions/plosive-pops/) (via search summary)
- [RoT] Microphone low-cut switches attenuate pops but "won't get rid of pops altogether"; on the AKG C414 (40/80/160 Hz positions) "80Hz or 160Hz setting is the usual choice for most vocals"; for mild pops, a very steep high-pass on the vocal track can suffice. — [Sweetwater inSync: Fix pop goes the vocal](https://www.sweetwater.com/insync/fix-pop-goes-vocal/) (via search summary)
- [RoT] A more aggressive plosive fix suggested on the Audacity forum is a high-pass near 250 Hz at 48 dB/octave, with the warning that above about 300 Hz "the voice will sound thin and tinny." — [Audacity Forum: Voice pop and de-esser filters](https://forum.audacityteam.org/t/voice-pop-and-de-esser-filters/49857) (via search summary)
- [RoT] Voice-EQ guides claim "almost nothing useful in a speaking voice lives below 80Hz." — e.g., [ProSoundWeb: How to EQ speech for maximum intelligibility](https://www.prosoundweb.com/how-to-eq-speech-for-maximum-intelligibility/2/) (via search summary; exact attribution among search results unverified)

#### High-pass / de-plosive — (d) position and (e) limits
- [DOC] iZotope: "De-plosive attempts to detect plosives between 20 to 80 Hz. If the input file has already been filtered with a high-pass filter, it is possible that plosive detection will not function correctly… it is recommended that De-plosive be used before applying a high pass filter." — [iZotope RX 9: De-plosive](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/de-plosive/index.html)
- [RoT] A plosive loud enough to clip the recording "cannot be repaired this way" (by filtering), because the waveform is already clipped. — [IRPR Sound: Plosive pops](https://sounddesign.irpr.agency/solutions/plosive-pops/) (via search summary)

### Inferences
- [INF] A notch series at f0, 2f0, 3f0… is literally a comb filter. That is why over-notching, or notching hum that is not there, sounds like the comb-filter/flanger coloration iZotope describes. Narrow (high-Q) notches limited to the harmonics actually visible in a spectrum minimize this.
- [INF] Because a 50/60 Hz fundamental lies below or at the bottom of the speech band (JBL: ~100 Hz up), a high-pass at ~80–100 Hz will also remove much of the hum fundamental, but not the harmonics at 100–400 Hz+. Those still need notches (or adaptive de-hum), and these harmonics overlap the 250–500 Hz region that carries most speech power.
- [INF] Where to set an HPF is a balance between removing rumble/pops and keeping low-voice warmth. The documented speech-range data (≈100 Hz lower bound) plus the 80–100 Hz rule of thumb are consistent, but no standard prescribes a cutoff.

### Gaps
- No standards-body or academic source prescribing an HPF cutoff for speech was found; voice fundamental-frequency ranges (male/female F0) were not retrieved from an authoritative source.
- Shure/Neumann microphone documentation on low-cut corner frequencies and proximity effect was not retrieved.
- Mouth-of-speaker "proximity effect" (bass boost at close range), a common reason for HPF on voice, was not sourced.

## 3. EQ for voice — which regions are adjusted (mud/boxiness, presence, sibilance, air) and why; documented guidance vs rule-of-thumb folklore

### Takeaway
What is documented: speech power is concentrated around 250–500 Hz (vowels), while intelligibility comes mainly from the weaker 1–4 kHz consonant region (the 2 kHz octave contributes about 30%). Sibilance sits around 4–10 kHz and can occasionally go as low as 1.5 kHz. The named EQ "zones" ("mud/boxiness 200–500 Hz", "presence 3–6 kHz", "air 10 kHz+") are practitioner rules of thumb whose boundaries differ from source to source. EQ is normally a post-repair, pre-dynamics or around-dynamics step, and presence boosts make sibilance worse.

### Cited Findings

#### Documented acoustics of speech (basis for EQ decisions)
- [DOC] "Maximum speech power is in the 250 and 500 Hz bands, falling off fairly rapidly at higher frequencies. Lower frequencies correspond to vowel sounds and the weaker upper frequencies to consonants." — [JBL Technical Note: Speech Intelligibility](https://proaudioencyclopedia.com/speech-intelligibility-a-jbl-professional-technical-note-technical-notes-volume-1-no-26/)
- [DOC] "upper frequencies contribute most to intelligibility, with the octave band centered on 2 kHz contributing approximately 30%, and the 4 and 1 kHz bands 25% and 20% respectively"; "restricting the higher frequencies to around 3.5 kHz will result in a loss of about 20% of the potential intelligibility"; data "may vary according to underlying experimental methods" (Fletcher 1929 telephone data flattens above 4 kHz; a reverberant-room experiment showed gains continuing higher). — [JBL Technical Note](https://proaudioencyclopedia.com/speech-intelligibility-a-jbl-professional-technical-note-technical-notes-volume-1-no-26/)
- [DOC] The "s" sound "is continuous over a wide, high frequency range extending beyond 12 kHz." — [JBL Technical Note](https://proaudioencyclopedia.com/speech-intelligibility-a-jbl-professional-technical-note-technical-notes-volume-1-no-26/)
- DPA: vowel energy "usually diminishes rapidly above approximately 1 kHz," while consonants lie "more specifically, in the 2 kHz-4 kHz frequency range." — [DPA Mic University: Facts about speech intelligibility](https://www.dpamicrophones.com/mic-university/facts-about-speech-intelligibility) (via search summary; page was under maintenance when fetched)
- [DOC] "the phonetic information of speech lies primarily in the shape of the spectral envelope, and the lowest-frequency peaks of the envelope identify the vowels uniquely." — [Aalto: Noise attenuation](https://speechprocessingbook.aalto.fi/enhancement/noise-attenuation)
- [DOC] Sibilance: "Sibilance and harsh frequencies typically happen anywhere between 4 and 10 kHz depending on the vocal and instrument. However, they can sometimes go as low as 1.5 kHz with certain vocalist and microphone combinations." — [iZotope: The dos and don'ts of de-essing](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/the-dos-and-donts-of-de-essing.html)
- Sound On Sound (Drawmer MX50 review): sibilance "is generally found between 4kHz and 8kHz," and the de-esser's cutoff is variable (800 Hz–8 kHz) to suit different voices. — [Sound On Sound: Drawmer MX50 review](https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/drawmer-mx50) (via search summary)

#### Rule-of-thumb EQ zones for speech (folklore; vary by source)
- [RoT] Search-surfaced voice-EQ guides give: warmth/boxiness ~200–500 Hz; "mud" ~250–750 Hz; boosting 100–250 Hz can sound "boomy"; presence ~3–6 kHz (one suggests boosting 4.5–6 kHz, warning too much gives "a thin lifeless sound"; another a modest 2–3 dB lift with a moderately wide bell); "S" between 5–7 kHz, 3–8 kHz or 5–8 kHz depending on the author; and boosting presence makes "ess" and "sh" sounds piercing, so a de-esser or dynamic EQ is the usual fix. — [ProSoundWeb](https://www.prosoundweb.com/how-to-eq-speech-for-maximum-intelligibility/2/); [MusicGuyMixing: Voice EQ](https://www.musicguymixing.com/voice-eq/); [Sage Audio: Top 10 vocal EQ tips](https://www.sageaudio.com/articles/top-10-vocal-eq-tips-2) (via search summary; per-claim attribution among these pages not verified)
- [RoT] One guide notes that cutting to roughly 500 Hz–5 kHz produces "boxy, phone-style" sound, i.e., boxiness can also mean *lack* of lows and highs, not just a mid bump. — (via search summary of the same group of voice-EQ guides; attribution unverified)

#### Dynamic EQ (frequency-specific, level-dependent EQ)
- [DOC] FFmpeg `adynamicequalizer` applies "dynamic equalization," with a detection threshold and a detection filter frequency (default 1000 Hz) that trigger the EQ. — [FFmpeg Filters: adynamicequalizer](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html#adynamicequalizer)
- [DOC] "the boundaries between de-essers, multiband compressors, and dynamic EQs continue to blur"; modern de-essers let bands be static, compressing or expanding, keyed to a sibilance sidechain. — [iZotope: The dos and don'ts of de-essing](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/the-dos-and-donts-of-de-essing.html)

#### Position and side effects
- [DOC] Audacity's generic order: effects other than notch/click removal (which go before) come after noise reduction. — [Audacity Manual: Noise Reduction](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/noise_reduction.html)
- [DOC] Filter phase side effects (stated for de-hum notches but applicable to EQ filters): linear-phase designs add latency and pre-ringing; minimum-phase designs only post-ring. — [iZotope De-hum](https://help.izotope.com/docs/rx-plug-in-pack/De-hum.html)

### Inferences
- [INF] The documented data explain the folklore. Cutting a few dB around 200–500 Hz ("mud/boxiness") reduces the band with the most power but relatively little intelligibility, which raises clarity at a given loudness. A gentle lift in 2–5 kHz ("presence") targets the region carrying ~75% of the octave-band intelligibility weight in the JBL data (1, 2, 4 kHz bands). Both are consistent with the physics, but the exact numbers are craft.
- [INF] Because presence and sibilance overlap (iZotope: sibilance can reach down to 1.5 kHz), presence boosts should usually be paired with or followed by de-essing. Boosts also raise any residual noise in that band, which argues for EQ after noise reduction.
- [INF] Boosting "air" (very high frequencies) raises hiss and sibilance. On band-limited sources (phone calls, low-bitrate files) there may be nothing there to boost.

### Gaps
- No authoritative (AES/standards) source for "air" band frequencies or for named "mud/boxiness/presence" zones was retrieved. Sound On Sound articles specific to speech EQ were not found by search. ANSI S3.5 (Speech Intelligibility Index) band-importance tables were not accessed directly.
- The DPA article could not be fetched in full (maintenance page), so its figures are from a search snippet only.

## 4. Dynamics and repair processing: compression, noise gate/downward expander, limiter, automatic leveling; de-essing; de-plosive; de-click/mouth-click; de-clipping; breath reduction

### Takeaway
Dynamics processors are level-dependent gain controls. Compressors turn down signal above a threshold by a ratio, with attack/release smoothing and make-up gain. Gates and expanders turn down signal below a threshold by a range or ratio. Limiters are fast, high-ratio ceilings, ideally true-peak. Automatic levelers apply slow, smoothed gain toward a target. The voice-repair tools are mostly specialized versions of these: de-essers are compressors keyed to sibilance, broadband or split-band/multiband. De-plosive and breath tools detect a sound class and attenuate it. De-click and de-clip detect damaged samples and replace them by interpolation, often autoregressive (AR) modelling. Most documentation warns that aggressive settings cause pumping, dullness, lisping-like ess loss, unnatural breath gaps, or "squashed" sound, and that clipping repair needs headroom.

### Cited Findings

#### Compression — (a) problem, (b) mechanism
- [DOC] "The Compressor effect reduces the dynamic range of audio. One of the main purposes… is to permit the audio to be amplified further (without clipping)." Controls: Threshold (level above which compression applies), Ratio (slope above threshold; e.g., threshold −10 dB, 10:1: a 0 dB input exits at −9 dB), Knee width (soft transition), Make-up gain. Smoothing: Lookahead (gain reduction starts before the threshold is crossed; latency compensated), Attack (reacting after crossing, causing temporary "overshoot"), Release (gradual recovery, causing "undershoot"). — [Audacity Manual: Compressor](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/compressor.html)
- [DOC] "Compressing the dynamic range of an audio signal necessarily means modifying its waveshape, which causes distortion"; time smoothing reduces it; "The effect of a particular setting may depend on the effect of another setting." — [Audacity Manual: Compressor](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/compressor.html)
- [DOC] FFmpeg `acompressor`: threshold default 0.125, ratio default 2 (range 1–20), attack default 20 ms, release default 250 ms, makeup, knee default 2.82843, detection "rms" by default ("mostly smoother") or peak, stereo link average/maximum, and a `mix` (dry/wet) control. "If a signal is compressed too much it may sound dull or 'dead' afterwards or it may start to 'pump'." — [FFmpeg Filters: acompressor](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html#acompressor)
- [INF] FFmpeg's linear defaults convert to threshold 0.125 ≈ −18 dBFS and knee factor 2.828 ≈ 9 dB (my arithmetic: 20·log10).

#### Compression — (c) typical voice settings [RoT]
- [RoT] Podcast/voice guides converge on ratios of about 2:1–4:1 for speech (one: 2:1–3:1 for business podcasts, avoid ≥6:1); attack advice varies widely (1–5 ms, 3–5 ms, or 10–25 ms "to avoid muffling initial consonants"); release ~100–200 ms to avoid pumping; one cites an NPR-derived 11 ms attack / 110 ms release; threshold advice includes "1 dB below the average level" or around −18 to −22 dB for conversational speech. — [The Podcast Host: compression](https://www.thepodcasthost.com/equipment/how-to-use-compression/); [Acast: vocal compression guide](https://www.acast.com/en/blog/a-guide-to-vocal-compression-for-podcasters); [Podcast Engineering School: attack & release](https://podcastengineeringschool.com/attack-and-release-times-for-compression/); [PodcastVideos: compression settings](https://www.podcastvideos.com/how-to-structure-audio-compression-settings-for-clear-corporate-podcast-vocals/) (via search summary; sources conflict on attack time)
- [DOC] Audacity ships voice-oriented compressor presets ("Fat", "Power", "Make it Right" for dialogs/podcasts; "Voice Memos Balance" for iPhone voice memos, which "will raise the volume of quiet sounds a little"). — [Audacity Manual: Compressor](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/compressor.html)

#### Compression — (d) position, (e) side effects
- [DOC] After noise reduction: "Do any compression… after doing noise reduction, not before." — [Audacity Manual: Noise Reduction](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/noise_reduction.html)
- [DOC] Compression raises low-level sounds relative to loud ones; Audacity even lists "attenuating louder parts of vocals to make breathing and other close-up sounds very audible" as a (creative) use. — [Audacity Manual: Compressor](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/compressor.html)
- [DOC] Apple warns that "content that is heavily compressed and amplified might be too loud, will lack the dynamic range, and can introduce distortion." — [Apple Podcasts: Audio requirements](https://podcasters.apple.com/support/893-audio-requirements)

#### Noise gate / downward expander
- [DOC] A gate passes audio above the threshold unaffected ("open") and "stops or reduces sounds below the threshold level" ("closed"); used to cut residual noise between phrases. Controls: Gate threshold; "Gate frequencies above" (gate only the band above a set frequency, up to 10 kHz, useful for hiss); Level reduction (a.k.a. "Range"; −100 dB = full mute); Attack (with look-ahead, so the gate is already open when speech starts); Hold (keeps it open briefly after level drops); Decay (a.k.a. release). Workflow: analyze the noise level, test with −100 dB, adjust the threshold, then reduce "Level reduction" only as much as needed: "For the most 'transparent' results avoid setting the Level reduction any lower than absolutely necessary." Deeper reduction "may also produce noticeable pumping." Unlinked stereo can make sound appear to move across the stereo field. — [Audacity Manual: Noise Gate](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/noise_gate.html)
- [DOC] FFmpeg `agate`: reduces the signal below a threshold by `ratio` (default 2), with the floor set by `range` (default 0.06125), attack 20 ms and release 250 ms, rms/peak detection; supports downward (default) or upward mode; the doc states that setting range to 0 disables reduction "and then filter behaves like expander." — [FFmpeg Filters: agate](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html#agate)
- [INF] FFmpeg's default `range` 0.06125 ≈ −24 dB (my arithmetic). With ratio 2 and a finite range, the default behaves like a downward expander with a floor rather than a hard mute.
- [DOC] Gating artifacts: lost word-initial plosives, odd on/off sound; hysteresis and fade ramps are needed; "muting the background noise entirely is not always desirable." — [Aalto: Noise attenuation](https://speechprocessingbook.aalto.fi/enhancement/noise-attenuation)

#### Limiter (incl. true-peak limiting)
- [DOC] A limiter passes signals below a level "unaffected or gently reduced, while preventing the peaks of stronger signals from exceeding this threshold"; it uses the same technology as the compressor and is "usually the last stage of mastering." A long release "will tend to lose quiet sounds that come after loud ones, but will avoid the volume being raised too much during short quiet sections like pauses in speech." Presets include "Master Limiter" (limits to −1 dB) and "VO Limiter… for dialogs, podcasts and everything else that has to do with voice." — [Audacity Manual: Limiter](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/limiter.html)
- [DOC] "Safety limiters to avoid overmodulation will have to be able to work in True-Peak mode and need to be adjusted to the appropriate Maximum Permitted True-Peak Level." — [EBU Tech 3343 (2023)](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/tech/tech3343.pdf)
- [DOC] Spotify's own playback limiter (used only in the "Loud" mode) engages "at -1 dB (sample values), with a 5 ms attack time and a 100 ms decay time." — [Spotify for Artists: Loudness normalization](https://support.spotify.com/us/artists/article/loudness-normalization/)
- [DOC] EBU: "Severe peak limiting and compression lead to a less dynamic, more squashed sound." — [EBU Tech 3343 (2023)](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/tech/tech3343.pdf)

#### Automatic leveling (slow gain riding)
- [DOC] FFmpeg `dynaudnorm` (Dynamic Audio Normalizer) computes a peak-based gain per frame (default 500 ms), smooths gains with a Gaussian window (default 31 frames, i.e., 15 before and 15 after), caps at a target peak (default 0.95, "5% headroom") and a maximum gain (default 10×) "to avoid excessive gain factors in 'silent' or almost silent frames"; optional RMS target and optional "traditional" compression. It "will 'even out' the volume of quiet and loud sections." — [FFmpeg Filters: dynaudnorm](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html#dynaudnorm)
- [DOC] FFmpeg `speechnorm` (Speech Normalizer) "expands or compresses each half-cycle of audio samples (local set of samples all above or all below zero and between two nearest zero crossings)" toward a target peak (default 0.95) with max expansion/compression factors and raise/fall rates ("Setting this options too high may lead to distortions"); presets range from "Weak and slow" (`e=3:r=0.00001:l=1`) to "Extreme and fast" (`e=50:r=0.0001:l=1`). — [FFmpeg Filters: speechnorm](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html#speechnorm)
- [DOC] Platform-side leveling exists too: YouTube's "Stable volume balances the range between quiet and loud, continuously adjusting volume levels to reduce variations in sound," is on by default, is "not available for all videos," and is turned off for YouTube Music/official music videos and when Voice boost is on. — [YouTube Help: Control video volume](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14106294?hl=en)

#### De-essing
- [DOC] "A de-esser is a type of audio compressor that applies gain reduction to sibilance"; detection typically 4–10 kHz. Types: **manual** (clip-gain sibilant regions down "a few dB" with crossfades); **wideband** (detect sibilance, "bring the whole signal down"); **split-band** ("reduce sibilance above a selected frequency, leaving the rest of the signal intact"); multiband/dynamic-EQ hybrids. — [iZotope: The dos and don'ts of de-essing](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/the-dos-and-donts-of-de-essing.html)
- [DOC] Two documented algorithms in RX: "Classic" detects sibilants and "attenuates them with a broadband gain envelope"; "Spectral" is "a multiband compressor with dozens of bands" attenuating only the high frequencies where sibilance is active and avoiding "modulation of ambient noise." Threshold can be *relative to speech level* (default) or absolute; the Cutoff frequency is the lower boundary of sibilance detection. Speed: too fast "can reduce the high frequency signal too much in the initial transient phase… reduces useful high frequency definition"; too slow causes "pumping in the high frequencies." — [iZotope RX 9: De-ess](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/de-ess/index.html)
- [DOC] FFmpeg `deesser`: intensity (trigger), amount of "ducking on treble part of sound" (default 0.5), how much original frequency content to keep (default 0.5), with outputs for the processed signal or the "ess" alone. — [FFmpeg Filters: deesser](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html#deesser)
- [RoT] With wideband de-essing, if words sound unnaturally dipped, lower the reduction to ~3–5 dB instead of 6–8 dB; split-band is often preferred as more precise. — [Unison: De-essing](https://unison.audio/de-essing/) (via search summary); [Sound On Sound: Drawmer MX50](https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/drawmer-mx50) (via search summary: split-band uses a crossover and compresses only the upper band)

#### De-plosive
- [DOC] RX De-plosive "can intelligently identify, separate and reduce plosives… while still preserving the fundamental frequency content and harmonics of the dialogue." Controls: Sensitivity (how much audio is classified as plosive; matters more than Strength; too high flags real speech); Strength (more reduction "at the cost of decreasing the quality and clarity of the speech signal"); Frequency limit (upper boundary; plosives 20–300 Hz, sometimes 500 Hz). Use before a high-pass filter. — [iZotope RX 9: De-plosive](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/de-plosive/index.html)

#### De-click and mouth-click removal
- [DOC] De-click repairs "clicks and glitches (short impulse noises)… including digital errors, mouth noises… or any other audio problem caused by impulses and discontinuities"; it "analyzes audio for amplitude irregularities and smoothes them out." Algorithms: single-band (narrow "digital" clicks), M-band periodic, M-band random (with protection for periodic elements like brass or vocals); click types: Click, Thump (low-frequency), Discontinuity (sudden waveform changes). Sensitivity too high "can repair too many intervals which can result in distortion"; "Clicks only" output to audition. — [iZotope De-click Plug-in](https://help.izotope.com/docs/rx-plug-in-pack/De-click.html)
- [DOC] Mouth De-click "detects and reduces mouth noises such as clicks and lip smacks"; "Increasing sensitivity can impact plosives, reducing or damaging the original signal"; Frequency Skew (≥0 targets mid-frequency mouth clicks); Click Widening extends repair around clicks with decay (lip smacks); two passes can help because loud clicks mask quieter ones. — [iZotope RX 9: Mouth De-click](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/mouth-de-click/index.html)
- [DOC] FFmpeg `adeclick`: "Samples detected as impulsive noise are replaced by interpolated samples using autoregressive modelling"; window 55 ms, overlap 75%, AR order 2% of window, threshold 2 (lower = more detections), burst fusion 2%; overlap-save mode leaves non-interpolated samples unchanged. — [FFmpeg Filters: adeclick](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html#adeclick)
- [DOC] Audacity Click Removal: Threshold (lower catches softer clicks but risks false detection) and Max Spike Width; needs >4096 samples; "Broader clicks of 10 milliseconds or longer may not be removed"; Repair works on ≤128 samples (~3 ms at 44.1 kHz). — [Audacity Manual: Click Removal](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/click_removal.html)
- [DOC] Interpolation of a broad thump "takes audio on either side of a transient and figures out what the audio in between would sound like"; low quality settings "mangle the audio," high settings were seamless in iZotope's test. — [iZotope: Order of operations](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/order-of-audio-repair-operations.html)

#### De-clipping (how clipped peaks are reconstructed)
- [DOC] De-clip "will process any audio above a given threshold, interpolating the waveform to be more round"; set the threshold "just under the level where the signal clips," located with a histogram (samples piling up at the top/bottom edges indicate clipping). De-clip "usually increases signal levels by interpolating signal segments 'above' the clipping point," so use makeup gain/post true-peak limiter, or store as 32-bit float (values above 0 dBFS are preserved but will clip at the DAC). — [iZotope De-clip Plug-in](https://help.izotope.com/docs/rx-plug-in-pack/De-clip.html)
- [DOC] Audacity Clip Fix "attempts to reconstruct clipped regions by interpolating the lost signal. It is only likely to be effective for repairing lightly clipped audio"; it assumes clipping at the same level for positive and negative peaks (apply DC-offset correction first if only one side is fixed) and reduces amplitude (default −9 dB) "to provide headroom for the clip-fixed reconstruction." — [Audacity Manual: Clip Fix](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/clip_fix.html)
- [DOC] FFmpeg `adeclip`: "Samples detected as clipped are replaced by interpolated samples using autoregressive modelling" (AR order default 8% of window); clipped samples are detected with a histogram (default size 1000). — [FFmpeg Filters: adeclip](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html#adeclip)
- [DOC] Academic framing: declipping estimates "the original audio signal, given its clipped measurements"; algorithms assume e.g. "sparsity or low-rankness" and are evaluated by signal-to-distortion ratio and perceptual metrics. — [arXiv 2007.07663: A survey and an extensive evaluation of popular audio declipping methods](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.07663)
- [DOC] "Of all the different forms of crud out there, nothing cuts deeper than clipping": fix it first, even inaudible clipping, because "later operations will magnify their damage," and choose "a pretty drastic gain reduction… to leave room for the reconstructed peaks." — [iZotope: Order of operations](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/order-of-audio-repair-operations.html)

#### Breath reduction
- [DOC] RX Breath Control "distinguishes breaths from dialogue or sung vocals based on their harmonic structure" rather than a threshold. **Gain mode** cuts all detected breaths by a fixed amount, which "can result in unnatural sounding results." **Target mode** only reduces breaths louder than a target level, so "quiet, natural sounding breaths will be left at the same volume." — [iZotope RX 9: Breath Control](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/breath-control/index.html)
- [RoT] Practitioners recommend lowering breaths rather than deleting them: removing them completely often sounds "unnatural and thus just as distracting"; Cubase's "Breath And Fade" macro lowers a breath and adds fades. A deleted breath can create a jarring edit, and over-removal makes a voice sound "robotic and clipped." If breaths are silenced, fill with room tone so the noise floor does not drop. — [Sound On Sound (Cubase podcast editing)](https://www.soundonsound.com/node/4921316); [SoundGirls: Avoid over-editing podcasts](https://soundgirls.org/how-to-avoid-over-editing-podcasts/); [IRPR Sound: Harsh vocal de-breath](https://sounddesign.irpr.agency/solutions/harsh-vocal-de-breath/) (all via search summary)
- [DOC] ACX narration rules require removing "extra sounds such as plosives, mic pops, mouth noise, mouse clicks, and outtakes" and 1–5 s of room tone at the start and end of each file. — [ACX Audio Submission Requirements](https://help.acx.com/s/article/acx-audio-submission-requirements)

### Inferences
- [INF] Compressor, de-esser, de-plosive and breath control are all detector-plus-gain designs. The differences are what the detector listens to (overall level, a high-frequency band, low-frequency bursts, breath-like spectra) and where the gain applies (wideband vs one band vs many bands). This is why the same failure modes recur: pumping from release timing, dullness from too-fast high-frequency gain changes, and loss of real speech when the detector is too sensitive.
- [INF] Click/clip repair differ from the rest because they *replace* samples (AR/interpolation) rather than scale them. That works only for short gaps relative to the AR model's window (FFmpeg default window 55 ms; Audacity's ~10 ms limit for click width), which explains why heavy clipping and long thumps resist repair.
- [INF] Since compression raises breaths, mouth noise and residual noise relative to speech (Audacity's own note), de-click, de-breath and noise reduction belong before compression. Otherwise the compressor's gain changes also modulate the noise floor.
- [INF] Platform-side leveling (YouTube Stable volume, on by default) means an upload's dynamics may be further altered at playback. That argues against relying on extreme dynamic range in voice uploads, but I have not tested how it interacts with pre-compressed speech.

### Gaps
- Original de-essing/compression literature (AES papers) on optimal speech settings was not found; typical compressor settings above are rule-of-thumb only and conflict on attack time.
- The classic AR interpolation paper (Janssen, Veldhuis & Vries, IEEE TASSP 1986) and Godsill & Rayner's *Digital Audio Restoration* were not retrieved; algorithmic details of de-click/de-clip beyond the tool docs are therefore not sourced.
- "Lisping" as a de-esser over-processing artifact is common folklore but no source was retrieved for it.
- Recommended LRA (loudness range) targets for speech/podcasts were not found in an authoritative source.

## 5. Classic dereverberation (spectral late-reverberation suppression; weighted prediction error/WPE) and why reverb is harder to remove than steady noise

### Takeaway
Reverberation is the voice convolved with the room's impulse response. Its late tail is the speaker's own earlier sound, decaying exponentially over hundreds of milliseconds to seconds. Classic single-channel methods (Lebart et al. 2001; Habets) treat the late tail as a non-stationary "noise" whose power is predicted from the past signal with an exponential-decay model set by T60, then remove it with spectral subtraction or MMSE-type gains, inheriting musical-noise artifacts. WPE (Nakatani et al. 2010; Yoshioka & Nakatani 2012) instead predicts the late reverberation in each STFT frequency bin from earlier frames, skipping a few frames as a guard interval, and subtracts that prediction, re-estimating the filter iteratively with variance normalization. Reverb is harder than steady noise because it is signal-dependent and non-stationary, it is convolutive rather than additive, and it spans far more than one analysis frame. Exact inversion would need a precisely known room response, which changes when the talker moves.

### Cited Findings

#### Why reverb is harder than steady noise
- [DOC] "The process of reverberation can be modeled as a filtering process: the speech signal is convolved by the impulse response of the acoustic channel"; deconvolution methods "require the RIR to be known precisely, and have been shown to be little robust to small changes in the RIR." — [Lebart et al. 2001](https://www.ee.columbia.edu/~dpwe/papers/LebBD01-ssdereverv.pdf)
- [DOC] "The problem of reverberation suppression differs from classical speech de-noising in that the 'reverberation noise' is non stationary." Reverb causes "overlap-masking": "the energy of previous phonemes is smeared over time, and overlaps following phonemes"; because of absorption, "the reverberated energy decays exponentially, with a time constant depending on the characteristics of the room." — [Lebart et al. 2001](https://www.ee.columbia.edu/~dpwe/papers/LebBD01-ssdereverv.pdf)
- [DOC] Time-scale mismatch: Lebart et al. take speech as quasi-stationary over "about 50 ms" while the reverberation times considered are "around 1 s." — [Lebart et al. 2001](https://www.ee.columbia.edu/~dpwe/papers/LebBD01-ssdereverv.pdf); STFT frame duration is "inherently limited by the nonstationarity of speech signals." — [arXiv 1509.00533](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.00533)
- [DOC] Perceptual effect: early reflections (arriving within ~30–35 ms, extendable to ~50 ms) fuse with the direct sound and can even aid intelligibility, but "Late reflections generally do not integrate with the direct sound and generally degrade intelligibility"; with 1 s reverberation, the loud "ba" of "back" decays only 18 dB and masks the following "ck" by 8–13 dB. — [JBL Technical Note](https://proaudioencyclopedia.com/speech-intelligibility-a-jbl-professional-technical-note-technical-notes-volume-1-no-26/)

#### Spectral late-reverberation suppression (statistical-model spectral subtraction)
- [DOC] Lebart, Boucher & Denbigh, "A New Method Based on Spectral Subtraction for Speech Dereverberation," *Acta Acustica united with Acustica* 87 (2001) 359–366: "a novel estimator of the non-stationary reverberation-noise power spectrum, based on a statistical model of late reverberation," tested on real RIRs with reverberation times of 0.34–1.7 s, "consistently show[ing] significant noise reduction with little signal distortion" and "significant improvements in speech recognition scores," with processed recognition scores of 65%–78% across the RIRs considered. Because the model depends only on the reverberation time, a characteristic of the room, the talker can move within the room without breaking it. — [Lebart et al. 2001](https://www.ee.columbia.edu/~dpwe/papers/LebBD01-ssdereverv.pdf)
- [DOC] Limitation: like other spectral subtraction, it leaves "residual musical noise" (their results note some musical noise remains). — [Lebart et al. 2001](https://www.ee.columbia.edu/~dpwe/papers/LebBD01-ssdereverv.pdf)
- Habets-style refinements: the room impulse response is modeled as white Gaussian noise times an exponential envelope with decay constant δ = 3·ln(10)/(T60·fs), with early/late components split at a sample index and treated as uncorrelated; the model is valid when the direct-path energy is much smaller than the energy of the reflections. Habets combined this with an MMSE log-spectral amplitude estimator; Habets, Gaubitch & Naylor (ICASSP 2008) cascade late-reverb spectral suppression with linear-prediction residual processing for early reverb. — [arXiv 1509.00533](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.00533); [Habets, Gaubitch & Naylor, ICASSP 2008](https://www.commsp.ee.ic.ac.uk/~ndg/papers/eh_icassp_2008.pdf) (via search summary)

#### Weighted prediction error (WPE)
- [DOC] Bibliographic: T. Nakatani et al., "Speech Dereverberation Based on Variance-Normalized Delayed Linear Prediction," IEEE Trans. Audio, Speech, and Language Processing, 2010, DOI 10.1109/TASL.2010.2052251; generalized in Yoshioka & Nakatani, "Generalization of multi-channel linear prediction methods for blind MIMO impulse response shortening," IEEE TASLP 20(10), 2012, 2707–2720. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/TASL.2010.2052251); [nara_wpe README](https://github.com/fgnt/nara_wpe)
- [DOC] "WPE is a compelling algorithm to blindly dereverberate acoustic signals based on long-term linear prediction"; background noise and reverberation are "the two main impairments in acoustic signal processing and far-field speech recognition." — [nara_wpe README](https://github.com/fgnt/nara_wpe)
- [DOC] In the reference implementation, WPE operates on the complex STFT and is parameterized by `taps` ("Filter order"), `delay` ("Delay as a guard interval, such that X does not become zero") and `iterations` (defaults in `wpe_v0`: taps=10, delay=3, iterations=3). Each iteration computes the inverse power of the current estimate (the variance normalization), builds a prediction filter from correlations of the delayed past frames, and subtracts the filtered past from the observation. — [nara_wpe source: wpe.py](https://github.com/fgnt/nara_wpe/blob/master/nara_wpe/wpe.py)

#### Tool behavior, settings and side effects (classic, profile-based)
- [DOC] A classic (learned) de-reverb "processes audio according to the reverberant/direct ratio (also known as wet/dry ratio)"; Learn estimates the wet/dry ratio per frequency and "the overall rate of decay"; Tail Length "is an approximation of RT-60"; best learned on a selection "that starts with some noise floor (or room tone), is several seconds long, and includes both the direct signal and its reverberant tails." Over-processing cues: decrease Tail Length "if reverb tails and noise floors sound over-processed, or if the processed audio sounds dull"; increase it "if reverb tails reappear." — [iZotope RX 9: De-reverb](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/de-reverb/index.html)
- [DOC] Boundary with ML: iZotope's Dialogue De-reverb "leverages a machine-learning algorithm," so it is outside the scope of classic DSP; its docs note that a higher sensitivity gives more reduction "at the cost of introducing artifacts and reducing dialogue clarity." — [iZotope RX 9: Dialogue De-reverb](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/dialogue-de-reverb/index.html)
- [DOC] Chain position: iZotope groups "unwanted reverberance" with the last "variable noise" class (with breaths, sibilance, plosives), after steady broadband noise. — [iZotope: Order of operations](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/order-of-audio-repair-operations.html)

### Inferences
- [INF] Steady noise is additive and statistically independent of the voice, so a noise print taken in a pause describes the noise under speech too. Reverb tails are made *from* the voice. They exist only after speech, change with every phoneme, and overlap the next phonemes in time and frequency, so no fixed "reverb print" exists, and any suppression gain also hits speech energy that resembles the tail.
- [INF] Because a 0.3–1.7 s tail spans dozens of 20–50 ms STFT frames, reverb cannot be treated as a per-frame multiplicative filter. WPE models it across frames (multi-frame linear prediction), and the statistical methods model it as an exponential decay across frames. Both need the T60 or prediction filter to be estimated blindly, which degrades with noise. That is one reason tool chains denoise before dereverberating.
- [INF] Classic dereverb is most effective on moderate, diffuse late reverb and on recordings with decent direct-to-reverberant ratio. Strong early reflections ("boxy"/comb-filtered room sound) are only partly addressed by late-reverb suppression (Habets' cascade adds a separate early-reverb stage), so close-miking remains the main prevention.

- Full text of Nakatani et al. 2010 and of the REVERB Challenge summary paper (Kinoshita et al., EURASIP JASP 2016) could not be retrieved (blocked); listening-test evidence on artifacts of WPE vs spectral methods for podcast-style speech is therefore not included.

## 6. Loudness normalization: ITU-R BS.1770 (LUFS/LKFS, K-weighting, gating, true peak), EBU R128 (−23 LUFS), platform delivery targets (Apple Podcasts, Spotify, YouTube, others), changes over time; peak vs loudness normalization

### Takeaway
BS.1770 defines loudness as K-weighted mean-square level summed over channels (LKFS = LUFS; 1 LU = 1 dB), integrated with 400 ms blocks and a two-stage gate (−70 LKFS absolute, −10 LU relative), plus a 4×-oversampled "true-peak" measure in dBTP. EBU R128 normalizes broadcast programmes to −23.0 LUFS with a −1 dBTP ceiling. Streaming and podcast platforms use louder targets: Apple Podcasts −16 LKFS ±1 dB with true peak ≤ −1 dBFS; Spotify plays at −14 LUFS by default (−11 Loud / −19 Quiet); AES TD1008 recommends −18 LUFS for speech; ACX uses RMS −23 to −18 dB with peaks ≤ −3 dB. YouTube publishes no LUFS figure, and third parties measure ≈ −14 LUFS with turn-down only. Peak normalization only aligns the highest sample, so equally peaked files can differ greatly in perceived loudness. That mismatch is why broadcasting moved to loudness normalization.

### Cited Findings

#### ITU-R BS.1770 (current: BS.1770-5, 11/2023)
- [DOC] Current edition is Recommendation ITU-R BS.1770-5 (11/2023), "Algorithms to measure audio programme loudness and true-peak audio level"; the version line lists editions in 2006, 2007, 2011, 2012, 2015 and 2023. — [ITU-R BS.1770-5 (PDF)](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I!!PDF-E.pdf)
- [DOC] K-weighting = a two-stage pre-filter: stage 1 "shelving filter" that "accounts for the acoustic effects of the head, where the head is modelled as a rigid sphere," and stage 2 a "simple high-pass filter" (the RLB, revised low-frequency B-curve); loudness LK = −0.691 + 10·log10 Σ Gi·zi (zi = mean square per channel; Gi channel weights, surround channels 1.41 ≈ +1.5 dB). "The constant −0.691… cancels out the K-weighting gain for 997 Hz"; a +1 dB level change gives +1 LKFS. — [ITU-R BS.1770-5](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I!!PDF-E.pdf)
- [DOC] Gating: "gating of 400 ms blocks (overlapping by 75%), where two thresholds are used: the first at −70 LKFS; the second at −10 dB relative to the level measured after application of the first threshold." — [ITU-R BS.1770-5](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I!!PDF-E.pdf)
- [DOC] True peak: "the maximum… value of the signal waveform in the continuous time domain; this value may be higher than the largest sample value." Steps: attenuate 12.04 dB (only needed for integer math), 4× oversample (48→192 kHz; 2× suffices at 96 kHz), low-pass, absolute value, convert to dB TP. — [ITU-R BS.1770-5](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bs/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I!!PDF-E.pdf)
- [DOC] LUFS and LKFS are equivalent names ("The EBU uses 'LUFS' which is compliant with international naming conventions"). — [EBU R 128 (2023)](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r128.pdf)

#### EBU R 128 family (current versions 2023)
- [DOC] R 128 recommends normalizing Programme Loudness to "a Target Level of −23.0 LUFS," with ±1.0 LU permitted "where attaining the Target Level is not achievable practically (for example, live programmes)," ±0.2 LU for measurement error in QC, and Maximum True Peak "shall not exceed −1 dBTP… during production"; programmes are measured "in its entirety, without emphasis on specific foreground elements such as speech." — [EBU R 128 (2023)](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r128.pdf)
- [DOC] History (changes over time): first published Feb 2010; Aug 2011 V2 "relative gate changed from −8 to −10 LU"; June 2014 V3 target tolerance changed to ±0.5 LU (except live); Aug 2020 V4 tolerance updates and R 128 s1/s2 references; Nov 2023 V5 adds s3, s4 and Tech 3401 references. — [EBU R 128 (2023)](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r128.pdf)
- [DOC] EBU Mode meter (Tech 3341): Momentary = 0.4 s sliding window (ungated), Short-term = 3 s (ungated), Integrated = BS.1770 gating. — [EBU Tech 3341 (2023)](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/tech/tech3341.pdf)
- [DOC] Loudness Range (Tech 3342): based on 3 s short-term windows (≥2.9 s overlap), absolute gate −70 LUFS and relative gate −20 LU, LRA = difference between the 10th and 95th percentiles of the distribution. — [EBU Tech 3342](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/tech/tech3342.pdf); LRA not recommended for programmes <1 min. — [EBU R 128](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r128.pdf)
- [DOC] Production guidance (Tech 3343, Nov 2023 V4 "substantial re-write"): true peak in production ≤ −1 dBTP; "anchor-based" normalization (e.g., on the dialogue) can optionally be used when Programme Loudness and Dialogue Loudness differ by >5 LU; streaming services "typically… use a higher Target Loudness Level than specified in Broadcast Loudness standards"; the −18 dBFS 1 kHz alignment tone reads −18 LUFS. — [EBU Tech 3343 (2023)](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/tech/tech3343.pdf)
- [DOC] Streaming supplement R 128 s2 (first published Aug 2020; rev. June 2021; Nov 2023): programmes "should be streamed unchanged, that is at −23.0 LUFS," with loudness metadata; if the broadcaster wants control of dynamic treatment, "the interim value for the Distribution Loudness Level should be in the range of −20.0 to −16.0 LUFS," and broadcasters should switch back to −23 once mobile devices have enough headroom/gain. It cites AES 71 and AES 77 and CTA-2075. — [EBU R 128 s2 (2023)](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r128s2.pdf)

#### Platform and distribution targets for voice content
- [DOC] **Apple Podcasts:** "we recommend that the audio signals are preconditioned so the overall loudness remains around -16 dB LKFS, with a +/- 1 dB tolerance, and that the true-peak value doesn't exceed -1 dB FS," measured per ITU-R BS.1770-5, done "before the encoding process" because codecs "might clip the signal if the recommended true-peak value is not respected"; with Sound Check, content with loudness metadata plays "at -16 dB." - [DOC] **Spotify (music/tracks page):** "We adjust tracks to -14 dB LUFS, according to the ITU 1770 standard," normalization is applied at playback and does not alter the file; positive gain is limited by headroom ("leave 1 dB headroom for lossy encodings," e.g., a −20 LUFS track with −5 dBFS peak is lifted only to −16 LUFS); Premium users can pick Loud (−11 LUFS, with limiter), Normal (−14) or Quiet (−19); masters should keep true peak below −1 dBTP, or below −2 dBTP if louder than −14 LUFS; the web player and third-party devices don't normalize. — [Spotify for Artists: Loudness normalization](https://support.spotify.com/us/artists/article/loudness-normalization/)
- [DOC] **YouTube:** the official Help Center documents "Stable volume" (on by default; continuously adjusts volume to reduce variations) but gives no LUFS target. — [YouTube Help: Control video volume](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14106294?hl=en)

Some sources dispute whether quiet content is ever raised. — [Wikipedia: Audio normalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio_normalization); [Production Advice: Stats for nerds](https://productionadvice.co.uk/stats-for-nerds/); [Youlean](https://youlean.co/?p=30813) (via search summary)

- **AES streaming guidance:** AES TD1008 recommends delivering speech at −18 LUFS, music at −16 LUFS, and mixed real-time streams at −17 LUFS (within the speech recommendation's +2 LU upper tolerance); it replaced the 2015 TD1004. Publication date is reported inconsistently (Sept 2021 vs "early 2022"). — [Telos Alliance: Understanding loudness for streaming audio](https://docs.telosalliance.com/docs/understanding-loudness-for-streaming-audio); [Production Advice: TD1008](https://productionadvice.co.uk/td1008/) (via search summary for date)

R 128 s2 (2023) cites AES 77. — [EBU R 128 s2](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r128s2.pdf)
- [DOC] **US broadcast TV** "is normally -24 LUFS" (vs EBU −23). — [Audacity Manual: Loudness Normalization](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/loudness_normalization.html). #### Mono vs stereo measurement quirk (relevant to voice)
- [DOC] Under R 128 "the 2 channel 'dual mono' track will measure at 3 dB higher than the 1 channel version, even though they sound identical"; Audacity's Loudness Normalization by default treats mono as dual-mono (+3 dB internally), which "differs from many other LUFS meters." — [Audacity Manual: Loudness Normalization](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/loudness_normalization.html); FFmpeg `loudnorm` has a `dual_mono` option (default false) for the same purpose. — [FFmpeg Filters: loudnorm](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html#loudnorm)

#### How loudness normalizers work in practice
- [DOC] FFmpeg `loudnorm`: "EBU R128 loudness normalization. Includes both dynamic and linear normalization modes," single- or two-pass; targets integrated loudness, LRA and max true peak; in dynamic mode it upsamples to 192 kHz to detect true peaks; linear mode (a pure gain change) requires measured values and reverts to dynamic if the target LRA is below the source LRA or if the gain change would exceed the target true peak. — [FFmpeg Filters: loudnorm](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html#loudnorm)
- [DOC] Audacity's Loudness Normalization (default −23 LUFS or RMS −20 dB) "is normally best performed as a final editing step prior to export." — [Audacity Manual: Loudness Normalization](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/loudness_normalization.html)

#### Peak normalization vs loudness normalization
- [DOC] Audacity Normalize sets the *peak* amplitude (default −1 dB) and can remove DC offset, with all selected tracks normalized to the same peak; Loudness Normalization instead targets LUFS. — [Audacity Manual: Normalize](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/normalize.html); [Audacity Manual: Loudness Normalization](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/loudness_normalization.html)
- [DOC] EBU's rationale: "peak normalisation of audio signals has led to considerable loudness differences between programmes and between broadcast channels," and these inconsistencies "are the cause of the most viewer/listener complaints." — [EBU R 128](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r128.pdf); Tech 3343 adds that under peak normalisation "the peak normalisation paradigm got abused and a loudness competition" followed. — [EBU Tech 3343](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/tech/tech3343.pdf)

### Inferences
- [INF] Practical voice deliverable: normalize to the platform's integrated target with a true-peak limiter at ≤ −1 dBTP, as the last step before encoding. Apple (−16 LKFS ±1) and AES TD1008/AES77 (−18 LUFS speech) are the only official *podcast/speech* figures found. Spotify's −14 LUFS is an official *playback* reference documented for music, and YouTube's −14 is a third-party measurement. A −16 LUFS / −1 dBTP master is therefore a defensible single master, since louder platforms will turn up only within headroom and turn-down-only platforms leave it untouched. This is an inference, not a platform rule.
- [INF] Hitting a louder target such as −14/−16 LUFS on very dynamic speech forces either compression/leveling or true-peak limiting, so loudness targets indirectly drive dynamics processing. EBU's −23 LUFS leaves much more headroom and needs less.
- [INF] The "−19 LUFS for mono" folklore is consistent with the 3 dB dual-mono measurement offset documented by Audacity, but which convention a given platform's meter uses is not documented in the sources found.

## 7. Recommended processing order for a voice chain, and what authoritative sources say about over-processing

### Takeaway
The best-documented ordering principle is iZotope's "deepest damage first": clipping, then stereo/phase problems, then clicks, then hum, then steady broadband noise, then variable noise (breaths, sibilance, plosives, reverb). Audacity's manual likewise puts DC-offset removal right after capture, notch and click removal before noise reduction, compression after noise reduction, and peak/loudness normalization at the very end. Apple adds that loudness/true-peak preconditioning precedes encoding. Every authoritative source warns against over-processing: use the least reduction that works, listen to what is removed, avoid hard gating and over-notching, and avoid heavy compression/limiting that "squashes" speech.

### Cited Findings

#### Documented ordering rules
- [DOC] iZotope's principle: "always try to tackle the deepest damage first," where "deepest" means "most pervasive or numerous" and affecting subsequent processing; the review list ends with "Broadband noise: first steady, then variable." Order demonstrated: (1) De-clip; (2) stereo/azimuth alignment; (3) clicks (De-click/Mouth De-click, then Interpolate for broad thumps); (4) hum (De-hum); (5) steady broadband noise (Spectral/Voice De-noise); (6) variable noise "mic bleed, wind gusts, sibilance, plosives, rustling, even unwanted reverberance." For breaths vs esses, try both orders. In their example, Breath Control first created phrase-end artifacts that De-ess made worse, so De-ess went first. — [iZotope: Order of operations in audio repair](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/order-of-audio-repair-operations.html)
- [DOC] Rationale ("mud flows downstream"): small amounts of noise/distortion get amplified by later gain stages, so "the cleaner you keep earlier stages in the chain, the less crud builds up"; also, "later operations will magnify their damage." — [iZotope: Order of operations](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/order-of-audio-repair-operations.html)
- [DOC] Audacity: Normalize "immediately after recording capture (or import)… should be used only to remove any DC offset," with amplitude adjustment "normally best performed as a final editing step prior to export"; notch filtering and click removal before Noise Reduction; compression and other effects after it; Loudness Normalization as a final step. — [Audacity Manual: Normalize](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/normalize.html); [Audacity Manual: Noise Reduction](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/noise_reduction.html); [Audacity Manual: Loudness Normalization](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/loudness_normalization.html)
- [DOC] Clip-repair preconditions: apply DC-offset correction before Clip Fix if only one polarity is reconstructed. — [Audacity Manual: Clip Fix](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/clip_fix.html); click removal "as long as significant clicks are removed first" before per-channel normalization. — [Audacity Manual: Normalize](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/normalize.html)
- [DOC] De-plosive before the high-pass filter. — [iZotope RX 9: De-plosive](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/de-plosive/index.html)
- [DOC] Loudness and true-peak "preconditioning steps need to occur before the encoding process." — [Apple Podcasts: Audio requirements](https://podcasters.apple.com/support/893-audio-requirements); keep true peak below −1 dBTP (−2 dBTP if louder than −14 LUFS) to avoid transcoding distortion. — [Spotify for Artists](https://support.spotify.com/us/artists/article/loudness-normalization/)
- [DOC] Limiters "are usually the last stage of mastering." — [Audacity Manual: Limiter](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/limiter.html)

#### What sources say about over-processing
- [DOC] Noise reduction: "Use the lowest value that reduces the noise to an acceptable level. Higher values than necessary… will result in damage to the audio that remains"; listen to the Residue; artifacts "can be even more objectionable than the original noise." — [Audacity Manual: Noise Reduction](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/noise_reduction.html)
- [DOC] Musical noise "is a non-natural distortion and therefore more annoying than an incomplete noise attenuation," so stop just short of it. — [Aalto: Noise attenuation](https://speechprocessingbook.aalto.fi/enhancement/noise-attenuation)
- [DOC] Eliminating musical noise by grossly overestimating the noise attenuates the spectrum "much more than would be necessary," which "can generate audible distortions." — [Cappé 1994](https://www.di.ens.fr/olivier.cappe/Publications/Self-archive/ephma94.pdf)
- [DOC] iZotope: heavy de-noise (up to 40 dB) has "a dire effect on voice quality" and is "mainly used in audio forensics"; de-noise artifacts at 18 dB were "phasey"/"hollow"; De-hum with no hum creates comb-filter "metal tube" sound; the Repair Assistant's automatic chains did worse than careful manual ordering in their test ("other times it misses things that your ears will catch"); mixing back "a tiny bit of untreated original audio to add realism" is a listed remedy. — [iZotope: Order of operations](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/order-of-audio-repair-operations.html)
- [DOC] Gates: avoid setting level reduction "any lower than absolutely necessary" (pumping). — [Audacity Manual: Noise Gate](https://manual.audacityteam.org/man/noise_gate.html); hard gating loses plosives and sounds odd. — [Aalto: Noise attenuation](https://speechprocessingbook.aalto.fi/enhancement/noise-attenuation)
- [DOC] Compression: too much makes audio sound "dull or 'dead'" or "pump." — [FFmpeg Filters: acompressor](https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html#acompressor); "heavily compressed and amplified" podcasts are too loud, lack dynamic range and can distort. — [Apple Podcasts](https://podcasters.apple.com/support/893-audio-requirements); severe peak limiting/compression yields "a less dynamic, more squashed sound." — [EBU Tech 3343](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/tech/tech3343.pdf)
- [DOC] Repair detectors: De-click sensitivity too high "can result in distortion." — [iZotope De-click](https://help.izotope.com/docs/rx-plug-in-pack/De-click.html); Mouth De-click sensitivity can damage plosives. — [iZotope RX 9: Mouth De-click](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/mouth-de-click/index.html); De-plosive Strength degrades "quality and clarity." — [iZotope RX 9: De-plosive](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/de-plosive/index.html); de-ess speed too fast dulls high frequencies, too slow pumps. — [iZotope RX 9: De-ess](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/de-ess/index.html); fixed-gain breath removal "can result in unnatural sounding results." — [iZotope RX 9: Breath Control](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/breath-control/index.html); de-reverb that is too strong sounds "over-processed" or "dull." — [iZotope RX 9: De-reverb](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/de-reverb/index.html)
- [RoT] Over-editing breaths/pauses makes speech "robotic and clipped"; lower rather than delete breaths. — [SoundGirls](https://soundgirls.org/how-to-avoid-over-editing-podcasts/); [IRPR Sound](https://sounddesign.irpr.agency/solutions/harsh-vocal-de-breath/) (via search summary)

### Inferences
- [INF] A synthesized classic voice chain consistent with all sources above:
  1. Prevent at capture (pop/wind filter, distance, gain staging) ([iZotope RX 9 De-plosive](https://s3.amazonaws.com/izotopedownloads/docs/rx9/en/de-plosive/index.html); [iZotope order](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/order-of-audio-repair-operations.html)).
  2. DC-offset removal.
  3. De-clip, with headroom.
  4. De-click / mouth de-click / interpolate.
  5. De-hum (notch/comb).
  6. Steady broadband noise reduction (profile-based, gentle).
  7. Variable problems: de-plosive, then high-pass; de-ess and breath reduction in whichever order sounds better; classic dereverb last within repair.
  8. Corrective EQ (cut mud, gentle presence).
  9. Compression/leveling.
  10. Final de-ess if presence EQ or compression exaggerated sibilance (common practice, unsourced).
  11. Loudness normalization to the platform target with a true-peak limiter (≤ −1 dBTP).
  12. Encode.

  The placement of HPF is the main point of disagreement. Many practitioners put it first (see the rules of thumb in §2), but iZotope recommends de-plosive before HPF. EQ placement relative to compression is not prescribed by any source found.
- [INF] The common thread on over-processing: each repair stage both removes a problem and adds its own small artifact, and later gain stages (compression, loudness normalization to −16/−14 LUFS) amplify residual artifacts and noise. That is why the sources stress light settings early in the chain and audition of the removed component (Residue / "Output noise/hum/clicks/breaths only").

### Gaps
- No AES/EBU standard prescribes a processing order for voice; the ordering evidence is from software documentation and an iZotope educational article (which is also vendor content).
- Peer-reviewed listening studies on cumulative artifacts of multi-stage voice chains were not found.
- Position of EQ vs compression, and of a final de-esser, is not specified by any authoritative source found.
