# Rights and YouTube-policy constraints for a monetized Korean channel ("INFJ입니다2") using popular lectures, TED talks and commencement speeches

Scope and method: All pages were checked live on 2026-11-06. Quotes were copied from the page text, mostly by downloading the raw HTML and extracting sentences, so they are verbatim. Items marked **UNVERIFIED** could not be opened. These notes summarize primary sources and are **not legal advice**. ## Q1. What does TED's usage policy allow and forbid: commercial or ad-supported use, translation and dubbing, clips, re-uploads, and licensing?

### Takeaway
TED makes talks available only under CC BY-NC-ND 4.0, and its policy counts "ad supported content" as commercial. The policy also forbids clips and truncated versions, translation or dubbing outside TED Translators, and re-uploading to other YouTube channels. Any commercial use needs a paid license, requested through TED's media-licensing form (allow at least two weeks). For a monetized channel, the CC license itself permits essentially nothing beyond TED's own sharing rules. Any use of footage or transcripts would have to rest on a statutory exception (see Q4 and Q6) or on a paid license.

### Cited Findings
- License: talks may be shared "under our Creative Commons license … CC BY–NC–ND 4.0 International, which means it may be shared under the conditions below" — [TED Talks Usage Policy](https://www.ted.com/about/our-organization/our-policies-terms/ted-talks-usage-policy)
- The NonCommercial term explicitly covers ads: "you cannot use TED Talks in any commercial context or to gain any type of revenue, payment or fee … or in exchange for payment of any kind, including in any ad supported content or format." — [TED](https://www.ted.com/about/our-organization/our-policies-terms/ted-talks-usage-policy)
- NoDerivatives covers dubbing and translation: "no derivative works are permitted so you cannot edit, remix, create, modify or alter the form of the TED Talks in any way. This includes using the TED Talks as the basis for another work, including dubbing, voice-overs, or other translations not authorized by TED." — [TED](https://www.ted.com/about/our-organization/our-policies-terms/ted-talks-usage-policy)
- Clips: "You may not edit TED Talks, or alter them in any way, including by sharing truncated versions or clips. This is essential for preserving the integrity of the speaker's ideas." — [TED](https://www.ted.com/about/our-organization/our-policies-terms/ted-talks-usage-policy)
- TED's FAQ entry "Can I translate TED content for my YouTube channel?" answers: "We do not permit individuals and/or organizations to translate or dub outside of the official TED Translator community, as modifying the content in this way is not covered under TED's Creative Commons license. Please also note TED and TED-Ed have official channels on YouTube, and our distribution policy does not permit re-uploading TED content onto other YouTube channels." — [TED](https://www.ted.com/about/our-organization/our-policies-terms/ted-talks-usage-policy)
- Uses outside TED's own platforms: "The use of TED content outside TED.com or TED's official YouTube channel is not covered by the Creative Commons license or TED's usage policy. Please submit your licensing request using our TED Talks media licensing request form." — [TED](https://www.ted.com/about/our-organization/our-policies-terms/ted-talks-usage-policy)
- Transcripts and subtitles "may be used under the same Creative Commons license in conjunction with the TED Talk video. Copyright on the transcripts is owned by TED and any edits, alternate usage rights or changes to these documents are not permitted without permission." — [TED](https://www.ted.com/about/our-organization/our-policies-terms/ted-talks-usage-policy)
- Sites that embed talks: sharing must not be "the central purpose of your site … not to use TED Talks as the main content for your site, which generates revenue or other sources of income (including from ads) that relies on content we are providing". Embedders "must use the TED embeddable player … Scraping video from TED.com is not permitted", and "You must attribute TED as the owner of the TED Talk and add a visible link back to TED.com" — [TED](https://www.ted.com/about/our-organization/our-policies-terms/ted-talks-usage-policy)
- Commercial licensing: "Using a TED Talk in a commercial context requires a license. This type of use is not limited to uses that generate profit … in a podcast, and more." Also: "Please note that no promotional applications of TED content are permitted. Licenses for commercial applications involve a fee, which supports TED's nonprofit mission." — [TED](https://www.ted.com/about/our-organization/our-policies-terms/ted-talks-usage-policy)
- Timing: "We ask for a minimum of two weeks to review and process all media requests." The page shows no last-updated date. — [TED](https://www.ted.com/about/our-organization/our-policies-terms/ted-talks-usage-policy)
- TED-Ed is different: videos "on YouTube.com/TEDEducation are made available under YouTube's standard policy, not Creative Commons." — [TED](https://www.ted.com/about/our-organization/our-policies-terms/ted-talks-usage-policy)
- The CC license yields to statutory exceptions. The CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 legal code says: "For the avoidance of doubt, where Exceptions and Limitations apply to Your use, this Public License does not apply, and You do not need to comply with its terms and conditions." It defines Exceptions and Limitations as "fair use, fair dealing, and/or any other exception or limitation to Copyright…", and NonCommercial as "not primarily intended for or directed towards commercial advantage or monetary compensation." — [CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-nd/4.0/legalcode.en)
- TEDx uploads appear CC-marked on YouTube (observation, not a TED statement). I searched YouTube for "TED talk" with YouTube's Creative Commons filter (`sp=EgIwAQ%3D%3D`). It returned 19 of 20 results from TED-branded channels, mostly "TEDx Talks" (e.g., [Ag1AKIl_2GM](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ag1AKIl_2GM), [1PV7Hy_8fhA](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1PV7Hy_8fhA)). The filter was calibrated against Blender's CC-BY "Big Buck Bunny", which it returned. — [YouTube CC-filtered search](https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=TED%20talk&sp=EgIwAQ%253D%253D). The only CC option YouTube offers is CC BY: "If you add a Creative Commons Attribution license to your video, it means other creators can reuse your work subject to the terms of the CC BY license." — [YouTube Help: Creative Commons](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2797468?hl=en)

### Inferences
- For INFJ입니다2, every TED-licensed route is closed: monetization counts as "ad supported" use, clips are banned, Korean subtitles or dubbing are banned, and re-uploading is banned. The open paths are (a) a paid TED license (fee; "no promotional applications"), (b) a narrow statutory quotation or fair-use exception under Korean law, which by its own terms the CC license does not override (see Q4), or (c) discussing ideas without TED footage or transcripts.
- TED's official Korean subtitles fall under the same BY-NC-ND terms and TED owns the transcript copyright. Pasting TED's Korean subtitle text into the channel's own video is therefore also unlicensed for commercial use.
- TEDx is a conflict to resolve before relying on it. TEDx uploads that appear to be CC BY on YouTube would on their face allow reuse with attribution, but TED's policy says TED content may not be re-uploaded or modified. Check the "License" field on each video and get written confirmation from TED or the event organizer before relying on CC BY. **[professional review]**

### Gaps
- TED publishes no TEDx-specific reuse policy that I could find. The CC BY marking on TEDx Talks uploads was observed only through the search filter: the watch-page HTML did not expose the license field, even for the calibration video.
- TED's license fees and whether it licenses individual YouTube creators are not public.
- Whether TED holds full rights from each speaker (TED says it is "the owner") cannot be checked, because speaker agreements are not public.

## Q2. Who owns commencement-speech recordings and texts? Do universities publish policies? Are there known takedowns?

### Takeaway
There are two layers of rights. The speech text is a literary work that normally stays with the speaker or their estate. Harvard's own publication of J.K. Rowling's 2008 address carries "Copyright J.K. Rowling", and David Foster Wallace's estate's copyright claim led to removal of the viral "This is Water" video in 2013. The recording belongs separately to whoever made it, usually the university. Speeches by US federal officials made in the course of official duties are public domain in the US, but a university-made recording of such a speech is still its own work. I could not verify a published, university-wide policy on reusing commencement videos for Stanford or Harvard.

### Cited Findings
- Harvard's text of Rowling's speech is headed "Copyright J.K. Rowling June 5, 2008 … 'The Fringe Benefits of Failure, and the Importance of Imagination' Text as delivered." — [Harvard Gazette](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2008/06/text-of-j-k-rowling-speech/)
- "A nine-minute truncated cinematic video adaptation of Wallace giving the speech was produced by The Glossary and published on YouTube and Vimeo in May 2013. It was well received, but was removed by Glossary on May 21, 2013, due to a copyright claim by Wallace's estate." The speech was also sold as a book: "This is water … Kenyon College. (1st ed.). New York: Little, Brown" (2009). — [Wikipedia, "This Is Water"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/This_Is_Water) (secondary source)
- A contemporaneous post on the Glossary video was updated: "UPDATE: The David Foster Wallace Literary Trust exemplifies everything that's wrong with copyright law." This is opinion, but it corroborates that the Trust acted. — [The Marginalian, 2013-05-09](https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/05/09/this-is-water-glossary/)
- Under US law, a speech is protected once fixed. The Copyright Office lists "An extemporaneous speech that has not been filmed or recorded" as an example of an unfixed work that is not protected. — [Compendium III, ch. 300](https://www.copyright.gov/comp3/chap300/ch300-copyrightable-authorship.pdf)
- Korean law lists lectures and speeches as literary works: 제4조①1 "소설ㆍ시ㆍ논문ㆍ강연ㆍ연설ㆍ각본 그 밖의 어문저작물" — [저작권법 (law.go.kr)](https://www.law.go.kr/법령/저작권법)
- Korean law separates the video layer from the underlying works. 제100조① presumes that the rights needed to exploit a 영상저작물 pass to the 영상제작자. 제100조② says: "영상저작물의 제작에 사용되는 소설ㆍ각본ㆍ미술저작물 또는 음악저작물 등의 저작재산권은 제1항의 규정으로 인하여 영향을 받지 아니한다." — [저작권법](https://www.law.go.kr/법령/저작권법)
- US federal works: "Copyright protection under this title is not available for any work of the United States Government…" (17 U.S.C. §105(a)). §101 defines such a work as "a work prepared by an officer or employee of the United States Government as part of that person's official duties." — [copyright.gov, Title 17 ch. 1](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap1.html); [LII §105](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/105)
- The Copyright Office says this "includes works created by the President; Congress; … or any other officer or employee of the U.S. federal government while acting within the course of his or her official duties". It adds that a work "may be registered if the work was prepared at that person's own volition and outside his or her official duties". — [Compendium III §313.6(C)(1)](https://www.copyright.gov/comp3/chap300/ch300-copyrightable-authorship.pdf)
- The White House: "Pursuant to federal law, government-produced materials appearing on this site are not copyright protected." Also: "Except where otherwise noted, third-party content on this site is licensed under a Creative Commons Attribution 3.0 License." — [whitehouse.gov/copyright](https://www.whitehouse.gov/copyright/)

### Inferences
- A foreign commencement speech can need two clearances: the speaker or estate for the words (a Korean translation is a derivative work, see Q4) and the university or videographer for the footage. Translating the text alone still needs the speaker's permission unless an exception applies.
- High-profile speeches that became books, such as Rowling's and Wallace's, carry active commercial interests and a real takedown history, so expect enforcement.
- A sitting US official's official-duty speech (for example, a presidential commencement address) is public domain in the US as text. Use government-produced recordings (White House or National Archives) rather than the university's footage, which is separately owned.

### Gaps
- **UNVERIFIED (pages returned HTTP 503 or TLS errors):** search summaries said a Stanford Digital Repository record for a 1994 commencement recording states it "may be used freely for non-commercial purposes with an attribution". They also said commercial use goes through University Archives (universityarchives@stanford.edu), that the Stanford Board of Trustees holds copyright in the commencement collection, and that a Stanford Medicine speaker-release form contemplates copyright for both speaker and university. Sources: [purl.stanford.edu/zg770ys4324](https://purl.stanford.edu/zg770ys4324), [Stanford commencement collection](https://purl.stanford.edu/xd234pk8792), [speaker release PDF](https://med.stanford.edu/content/dam/sm/rsl/docs/speakerrelease_final.pdf).
- I found no Harvard policy on reusing commencement video. Ownership of Steve Jobs's 2005 Stanford text and the license on Stanford's video were not verified (news.stanford.edu blocked access).
- I found no primary document of the estate's claim (for example, a DMCA notice). The takedown facts come from Wikipedia and a blog.
- Whether Korea treats US federal works as unprotected is unsettled. Korean 제7조 excludes laws and notices of "국가 또는 지방자치단체" but has no clause like §105 for foreign governments. **[professional review]**

## Q4. What do Korean law and government guidance say about translating and quoting speeches (2차적저작물, 제28조, 공정이용 article number, 주종관계)?

### Takeaway
Under the current Act (시행 2026. 10. 29.), lectures and speeches are protected literary works. Translating one creates a 2차적저작물 that only the author may make or authorize (제5조, 제22조), and the culture ministry (문화체육관광부) says translation requires the original author's prior permission. Quotation is allowed only "정당한 범위 안에서 공정한 관행에 합치되게" (제28조). The Supreme Court requires a 주종관계, in which your own work is primary and the quoted part subordinate, and it weighs purpose, nature, amount, method, reader perception and market substitution (2011도5835). A quoted portion may be translated (제36조②), and the source must be credited (제37조). The general fair-use clause is now **제35조의5** (moved from 제35조의3 on 2019. 11. 26.). Publicly delivered political speeches are freely usable (제24조), but other speeches are not. Penalties rose in 2026: up to 5x damages for intentional infringement and up to 7 years or ₩100 million.

### Inferences
- Korean subtitles or dubbing of a whole foreign speech is 2차적저작물 작성 and needs permission. 제36조② allows translating only what may lawfully be quoted under 제28조, not the whole speech.
- Under 주종관계, a video where the speech excerpt dominates (for example, a long clip with a little commentary) is unlikely to qualify. The channel's own analysis must be the main body and the quote supporting material. A video that lets viewers skip the original fails the "원저작물에 대한 수요를 대체" factor.
- 제35조의5 is a residual safety valve with no bright lines. Monetization is weighed under "이용의 목적 및 성격" and the market-effect factor; the statute does not say how heavily. **[professional review]**
- 제24조 may cover publicly delivered political speeches (for example, a president's or politician's public address). It does not cover commencement speeches or lectures in general. Broadcaster footage of such a speech is plausibly a separate work, so 제24조 covers the words, not someone else's video.
- For a monetized channel, the 2026 changes (up to 5x damages for intentional infringement, prosecution without a complaint for for-profit infringement under 제140조) raise the cost of getting this wrong.

## Q5. What does YouTube's monetization policy say about reused content, "inauthentic content" and reading other people's material, and what does YouTube's fair-use page say?

### Takeaway
To be monetized, content must be original and "authentic". Channels that repurpose existing content without "significant original commentary, substantive modifications, or educational or entertainment value" are demonetized as reused content, and this applies channel-wide. It applies "even if you have permission", because it is "not based on copyright, permission, or fair use". "Content that exclusively features readings of other materials you did not originally create" is expressly non-monetizable. On 2025-07-15 YouTube renamed "repetitious content" to "inauthentic content" (Korean page: '양산형 콘텐츠') to cover mass-produced and template or AI-generated content, and left the reused-content policy unchanged. YouTube's fair-use page says credit and disclaimers don't create fair use, that Content ID claims block monetization even for seconds, and that rules differ by country.

### Inferences

## Q6. Which practical approaches are lower-risk, and what remains uncertain?

### Takeaway
From lowest to highest risk:
1. **Original commentary on the ideas**, paraphrased, with no footage, audio or transcript copied. Copyright protects expression, not ideas, and this format is also the best fit for YouTube's originality rules.
2. **Short quotations inside substantial original analysis**, credited by name and source. The quoted part may be translated. This rests on 제28조 and 주종관계, but TED, 정토회 and 김창옥TV publicly reject unlicensed reuse, so expect claims and disputes.
3. **Public-domain or freely usable sources**, such as US federal officials' official-duty speeches with government-made recordings, or Korean public political speeches (제24조) and public works (제24조의2). The reused-content policy still applies to these.
4. **Licensing** through each owner's channel: TED's paid form, 정토회's written request, and the business emails of 세바시, 김창옥 and MKTV. CJ ENM has no published route.

Full translated re-uploads, dubbing and clip compilations are high-risk on both the copyright and the monetization side.

### Cited Findings
- Ideas are not protected: "In no case does copyright protection for an original work of authorship extend to any idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation, concept, principle, or discovery, regardless of the form in which it is described, explained, illustrated, or embodied in such work." (17 U.S.C. §102(b)) Korea defines a work as an "표현한 창작물" (제2조1). — [copyright.gov](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap1.html); [law.go.kr](https://www.law.go.kr/법령/저작권법)
- Quotation route: 제28조, plus 제36조② (translation of the quoted part) and 제37조 (attribution), applied under the 주종관계 test of 2011도5835 (Q4). — [law.go.kr](https://www.law.go.kr/법령/저작권법); [nepla.ai](https://www.nepla.ai/case/대법원/2011도5835)

### Inferences
These are practical rules derived from the sources above, not legal advice.
- Build each video around the channel's own thesis, for example "what this speech's idea means for INFJs". Speech material should be brief and supporting. Avoid uploading content whose main value is the speech itself.
- Prefer short text quotes in the creator's own Korean translation over clips. Never reuse TED's official subtitles. Credit every quote by speaker, event and year, with a link (제37조).
- Treat clips from TED, 세바시, 어쩌다 어른, 즉문즉설 and 김창옥TV as likely to draw claims. A claim stops monetization of that video even when the use may be lawful. Disputes and appeals are possible, but YouTube's legal-cost protection is US-only.
- For a series on a single source, such as 즉문즉설 or 김창옥, seek written permission first. 정토회 already has a defined process.
- Public-domain speeches still need original commentary to stay monetizable, because the reused-content policy is "not based on copyright".
- Use government-made recordings, not university or broadcaster footage, of a US official's speech. Keep a permissions log.
