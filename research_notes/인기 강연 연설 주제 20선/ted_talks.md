# Most popular TED & TEDx talks (all-time vs. 2024–2026) and the themes they cover

All view counts were checked on **2026-11-06**. Count sources: TED.com `viewedCount` from the talk or playlist page's embedded data; YouTube exact counts from the watch-page data (youtubei `next` endpoint) or YouTube search results. Where only YouTube's rounded channel-listing figure was available, the number is marked **"abbrev."** "Official" means an upload on the TED, TEDx Talks or TED-Ed YouTube channels. Every other channel is marked as a **re-upload**, and its licensing was not verified.

## Q1. What are TED's official "most popular talks of all time," and what are their current view counts?

### Takeaway
TED's official playlist ranks Sir Ken Robinson's *Do schools kill creativity?* first, at 80.7M views. Tim Urban (79.3M) and Amy Cuddy (77.9M) follow. Psychology and self-improvement talks dominate the list: by my count, about 14 of the 25 are squarely in that category and about 7 more touch on psychological themes. On YouTube alone the order differs. On TED's own channel, Tim Urban (62.2M), James Veitch (58.4M) and Julian Treasure (52.0M) lead, and Ken Robinson is only about 13th (25.1M).

### Inferences
- The two rankings measure different audiences. TED.com aggregates favor early-TED.com-era talks such as Robinson, Taylor, Pink and Adichie, where TED.com supplies 40–60% of views. YouTube favors short, funny, practical talks: Urban, Veitch, Treasure.
- For a YouTube channel, the YouTube-native ranking is the better gauge of what YouTube audiences click.
- TED.com counts seem to fold in YouTube views of the official upload. Urban's 79.3M × 79% ≈ 62.7M, close to his 62.2M on YouTube. Sam Berns is an exception: TED.com shows 50.0M, while YouTube already has 54.7M. The YouTube portion of TED.com counts may therefore update with a lag.
- The playlist order is stale against current counts. By today's numbers, Treasure (71.75M) would rank above Brown (71.44M) and Sinek (71.36M), and Adichie (43.4M) would rank above Roach, Russell, Shaw and Thum.
- Counting splits across uploads: Sinek has about 36.7M on YouTube across the TED and TEDx copies, and Brown about 30.8M.

### Gaps
- TED does not publicly document how it counts views (whether it dedupes, or how it handles embeds and partners). The only methodology visible is the platform-share fields.
- The YouTube figure for Jill Bolte Taylor is an abbreviated channel-listing number.
- YouTube re-uploads of most top talks were not searched exhaustively. Only Robinson, Cuddy, Brown, Sinek, Gilbert, Achor and Hari were searched for re-uploads.

  - TED's own page note on the talk: "(NOTE: Some of the findings presented in this talk have been referenced in an ongoing debate among social scientists about robustness and reproducibility. Read 'Corrections & Updates' below…)." The page links a "Corrections & Updates" page and Cuddy's reply. — [TED.com talk](https://www.ted.com/talks/amy_cuddy_your_body_language_may_shape_who_you_are); [TED criticisms/updates page](https://www.ted.com/pages/amy-cuddy-s-your-body-language-may-shape-who-you-are-criticisms-updates)
  - The official YouTube description carries the same note. — [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ks-_Mh1QhMc)
  - The original study is Carney, Cuddy & Yap (2010), *Psychological Science*. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610383437)
  - Ranehill et al. (2015): "Assessing the robustness of power posing: no effect on hormones and risk tolerance in a large sample of men and women." — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25810452/)
  - Co-author Dana Carney: "I do not believe that 'power pose' effects are real… The evidence against the existence of power poses is undeniable." She also called the original data "flimsy" with a "tiny" sample. — [Carney statement (PDF)](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/dana_carney/pdf_My%20position%20on%20power%20poses.pdf)
  - Simmons & Simonsohn's p-curve analysis ("Power Posing: P-Curving the Evidence," 2017). Data Colada's blog post on it is titled "Power Posing: Reassessing The Evidence Behind The Most Popular TED Talk." — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28485698/); [Data Colada [37]](https://datacolada.org/37)
  - Cuddy, Schultz & Fosse (2018) reply that a broader p-curve of postural-feedback research "reveals clear evidential value for power-posing effects." — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29498906/)
  - A 2017 *Comprehensive Results in Social Psychology* issue asked "Power poses – where do we stand?" — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/23743603.2017.1342447)
  - A 2022 meta-analysis of 73 studies found robust affective and behavioral differences between expansive and contractive postures. The effect was driven by the *absence of contractive* posture (contractive vs. neutral, g = 0.45), not by expansive posture (expansive vs. neutral, g = 0.06). — [Elkjær et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32569503/)
  - Media coverage: [Quartz](https://qz.com/804113/the-rise-and-fall-of-power-poses-gave-me-a-firsthand-look-at-the-dangers-of-psychologys-replication-crisis); [Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/science/power-pose-effects-arent-real-says-co-author-original-study); [CBC Quirks & Quarks](https://amp.cbc.ca/radio/quirks/sept-23-2017-1.4302012/no-evidence-at-all-that-power-posing-will-get-you-a-job-1.4302014)
- **Grit (Duckworth).** A meta-analysis of 584 effect sizes (88 samples, 66,807 people) found:
  - the "higher order structure of grit is not confirmed"
  - grit is "only moderately correlated with performance and retention"
  - grit is "very strongly correlated with conscientiousness"
  - the perseverance facet outperforms consistency of interest
  - "interventions designed to enhance grit may only have weak effects."
  - Source: [Credé, Tynan & Harms 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27845531/)
- **Growth mindset (Dweck; also Crum's TEDx).**
  - Macnamara & Burgoyne (2023), 63 studies, N = 97,672: overall d = 0.05, nonsignificant after correcting for publication bias. The highest-quality studies gave d = 0.02 (nonsignificant). Authors with a financial incentive reported larger effects. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36326645/)
  - Burnette et al. (2023), 53 samples, for targeted subsamples with high-fidelity implementation: academic achievement d = 0.14, mental health d = 0.32. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36227318/)
  - Further context: Sisk et al. (2018) ran two meta-analyses ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29505339/)), and a national experiment was published in *Nature* in 2019 ([Yeager et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31391586/)).
- **Stress mindset (McGonigal).**
  - Her headline finding comes from Keller et al. (2012). The study linked 1998 National Health Interview Survey data (n = 28,753) to deaths through 2006. Only people who reported *a lot* of stress *and* believed stress affected their health "a lot" had a 43% higher risk of premature death (HR = 1.43). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22201278/)
  - The study is observational. TED's description phrases the finding causally: stress "may only be bad for you if you believe that to be the case." — [TED.com](https://www.ted.com/playlists/171/the_most_popular_ted_talks_of_all_time)
- **Lie detection (Meyer).**
  - A meta-analysis of 206 documents and 24,483 judges found 54% correct lie/truth judgments overall: 47% of lies and 61% of truths correctly classified. — [Bond & DePaulo 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16859438/)
  - TED's description of Meyer's talk says "we're lied to from 10 to 200 times" a day. That figure was not verified. — [TED.com](https://www.ted.com/playlists/171/the_most_popular_ted_talks_of_all_time)
- **Synthetic happiness and the free-choice paradigm (Gilbert).** Chen & Risen argue that the free-choice paradigm "will produce spreading, even if people's attitudes remain unchanged." That paradigm is the classic evidence for choice-induced preference change. — [Chen & Risen 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20658837/)
- **Rat Park (Hari's addiction talk).** A 1996 replication attempt is titled "Environment is not the most important variable in determining oral morphine consumption in Wistar rats." — [Petrie 1996](https://doi.org/10.2466/pr0.1996.78.2.391)
- **Sleep (Walker's talk; the critique targets his book).** Alexey Guzey, "Matthew Walker's 'Why We Sleep' Is Riddled with Scientific and Factual Errors." — [guzey.com](https://guzey.com/books/why-we-sleep/)
- **Daniel Amen's SPECT scans (TEDx, 24.5M).** Skeptic outlets argue SPECT-guided psychiatric diagnosis lacks validation. — [Science-Based Medicine](https://sciencebasedmedicine.org/dr-amens-love-affair-with-spect-scans/); [SkepDoc](https://www.skepdoc.info/a-skeptical-view-of-spect-scans-and-dr-daniel-amen/)
- **Left/right brain (relevant to Bolte Taylor's framing).** A resting-state fMRI study of 1,011 people tested whether individuals are "left-brain dominant or right-brain dominant." — [Nielsen et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23967180/)
- **Field-wide context.** The Open Science Collaboration's "Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science" (*Science*, 2015) is the reference point for the replication crisis. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26315443/)

### Inferences
- The hits with the most psychology content are also the most contested. A recurring format could pair the talk with "what the science says now": power pose (2010 → 2015–2022), grit (2013 → 2017), mindset (2014 → 2023), stress belief (2013 → observational data). This gives retention-friendly twists and protects channel credibility.
- **Best fits for an INFJ, introvert-leaning audience:**
  - introversion and personality (Cain, Little)
  - vulnerability and shame (Brown)
  - loneliness and social health (Waldinger, Killam, Epley)
  - not taking things personally and emotional first aid (Imbo, Winch)
  - self-talk (Kross)
  - heartbreak and relationships (Winch, Perel)

  All of these combine high views with relatively solid or uncontroversial grounding.
- **Weaker fits:** spectacle or performance talks (Thum, Veitch, Blaine, Musk) and talks TED has flagged for medical claims. The flagged ones are usable only critically.
- No MBTI-centric talk appears anywhere in these top lists. Personality content would bridge best through the trait research Cain and Little discuss (my inference; the MBTI evidence debate was not researched here).

### Gaps
- The source results were not re-read for Sisk et al. 2018, Yeager et al. 2019, Nielsen et al. 2013, Deci et al. 1999 or the Open Science Collaboration 2015. Only titles and designs are cited above.
- No sources were gathered for critiques of Brown's qualitative method, Sinek's "limbic brain" claims, Robinson's creativity claims, Achor's "happiness advantage," or Hari's depression talk.
- The criticism of Amen comes from skeptic outlets. The Washington Post article commonly cited could not be retrieved, and no reply from Amen was gathered.

### Inferences
- The talks with clear Korean "afterlife" are psychology talks: Brown (two publisher re-uploads tied to her Korean book title 『나는 왜 내 편이 아닌가』), McGonigal, Urban, Cain (re-upload by the publisher of 『콰이어트』) and Cuddy. These match the channel's psychology focus.
- Korean channels may be seeing book-linked demand more than raw TED fandom.
- Re-uploads stay under 1M while official uploads are far larger. Korean viewers most likely watch official videos with Korean subtitles or localized titles.
- A Korean-language explainer of a famous talk therefore competes with the original's subtitles. It should add something: an evidence update, Korean context or a summary.
- Korean demand also uses TED as English-learning material, which suggests a "learn English with a psychology TED talk" format may be viable.
