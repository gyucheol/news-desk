## 1. Kroese, De Ridder, Evers & Adriaanse (2014)

- **Citation:** Kroese FM, De Ridder DTD, Evers C, Adriaanse MA. Bedtime procrastination: introducing a new area of procrastination. *Frontiers in Psychology*. 2014;5:611 (published 19 Jun 2014).
- **IDs:** PMID 24994989 · PMCID PMC4062817 · DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00611
- **Read:** PubMed abstract + full text (PMC).
- **Design / N:** Cross-sectional online survey. Recruited via Amazon Mechanical Turk ("community sample" in the abstract). 203 completed the survey. 7 with sleep disorders and 19 night-shift workers were excluded, leaving **N = 177** (48.6% male, mean age 37.9).
- **Definition (abstract):** "Bedtime procrastination is defined as failing to go to bed at the intended time, while no external circumstances prevent a person from doing so."
- **Key results:**
  - The authors built a 9-item Bedtime Procrastination Scale: "A 9-item scale was developed to assess bedtime procrastination (Cronbach's α = 0.92…)".
  - "Bedtime procrastination was negatively associated with self-regulation."
  - "self-reported bedtime procrastination was related to general reports of insufficient sleep above and beyond demographics and self-regulation."
  - Full text: "almost 30% of the sample reported to sleep only 6 h or less on week nights". "about 84% reported to feel having slept too little or to feel tired during the day at least once a week".
  - Final regression models explained "27.1% for hours of sleep, 27.8% for fatigue and 40.9% for experienced insufficient sleep."
  - Correlation of bedtime procrastination with general procrastination: r = 0.60. With sleep outcomes: "ranging from r = 0.46 to 0.61".
- **Limitation (authors):** "the cross-sectional nature of our study does not allow for causal inferences."
- **Verdict: correct.** For the script, say "associated with", not "causes".

## 2. Kroese, Evers, Adriaanse & de Ridder (2016)

- **Citation:** Kroese FM, Evers C, Adriaanse MA, de Ridder DTD. Bedtime procrastination: A self-regulation perspective on sleep insufficiency in the general population. *Journal of Health Psychology*. 2016;21(5):853–862 (epub 4 Jul 2014).
- **IDs:** PMID 24997168 · DOI 10.1177/1359105314540014
- **Read:** PubMed abstract + full text (publisher version PDF, Utrecht University Repository, handle 1874/346688).
- **Design / N:** Members of the LISS panel ("a representative sample of Dutch individuals"; a probability sample of households). Design: baseline survey plus a **7-day bedtime diary 1 month later**.
  - 2,637 completed the baseline survey and 2,106 also completed the diary.
  - 206 were excluded (night shifts or treatment for sleep problems).
  - Abstract: "a representative sample of Dutch adults (**N = 2431**)". The sample was 54.5% female, mean age 50.7.
- **Key results:**
  - Abstract: "a large proportion of the general population experiences getting insufficient sleep and regularly goes to bed later than they would like to … a relationship between self-regulation and experienced insufficient sleep was found, which was mediated by bedtime procrastination."
  - "a roughly similar percentage (53.1%) reported going to bed later than they want to on 2 or more days per week". Over 50% felt they had insufficient sleep on ≥2 days/week. "almost 45%" felt tired during the day on ≥2 days/week.
  - "Bedtime procrastination was the strongest predictor (β = .40, p < .001)" of experienced insufficient sleep. The full model explained 29.3% of variance.
  - Mediation was **partial**: "the effect of self-control on insufficient sleep was partially mediated by bedtime procrastination". Standardized indirect effect, 95% CI [−0.19, −0.15].
  - The most frequent pre-bed activity was watching TV, "ranging from 55.3% on Saturday to 65.5% on Monday night".
  - Bedtime procrastination scale in this study: 9 items, α = .88, test–retest r = .79.
- **Limitation (authors):** "despite the prospective design, it is not possible to make conclusions about the actual causal relationships".
- **Verdict: correct.** N = 2,431, and bedtime procrastination (partially) mediated the self-regulation → sleep-insufficiency link.

## 3. Exelmans & Van den Bulck (2017)

- **Citation:** Exelmans L, Van den Bulck J. Bedtime, shuteye time and electronic media: sleep displacement is a two-step process. *Journal of Sleep Research*. 2017;26(3):364–370 (epub 8 Mar 2017).
- **IDs:** PMID 28271575 · DOI 10.1111/jsr.12510
- **Read:** PubMed abstract only. The open repository copy (KU Leuven Lirias) could not be reached: the connection was reset and the record redirects to a JavaScript discovery page. Full-text details are therefore not confirmed.
- **Design / N:** Online survey, convenience sample, cross-sectional: "A convenience sample of 338 adults (aged 18–25 years, 67.6% women)".
- **Key results (abstract):**
  - The authors "separated bedtime (i.e. going to bed) from shuteye time (i.e. attempting to go to sleep once in bed)".
  - "Results indicated a gap of 39 min between bedtime and shuteye time, referred to as 'shuteye latency'."
  - Shuteye latency of "≤30 min, ≤1 or >1 h" → "3.3, 6.1 and 9.3 times more likely to be rated as poor sleepers" than going to sleep immediately.
  - Weekly time before bedtime: media 17 h 55 min vs non-media 14 h. After bedtime: media 3 h 41 min vs non-media 7 h 46 min.
  - "**Shuteye latency was related exclusively to prebedtime media use.**"
  - Conclusion: "respondents not only engage in the delay of bedtime, but also in the delay of shuteye time once in bed."
- **Verdict: partly.** The two-step idea (bedtime is delayed, then shuteye time is delayed again once in bed) is correct.
  - But the abstract links shuteye latency **only to media use before going to bed**. In-bed time was mostly *non-media* activity.
  - So "media use in bed delays falling asleep" is **not** what the abstract says.
  - It is also a cross-sectional survey, so "delays" is the authors' interpretation, not a demonstrated causal effect.

## 4. Chang, Aeschbach, Duffy & Czeisler (2015)

- **Citation:** Chang AM, Aeschbach D, Duffy JF, Czeisler CA. Evening use of light-emitting eReaders negatively affects sleep, circadian timing, and next-morning alertness. *PNAS*. 2015;112(4):1232–1237 (epub 22 Dec 2014).
- **IDs:** PMID 25535358 · PMCID PMC4313820 · DOI 10.1073/pnas.1418490112
- **Read:** PubMed abstract + full text (PMC HTML). The abstract gives no N and no numbers.
- **Design / N:**
  - Randomized crossover, 14-day inpatient protocol: "Twelve healthy young adults (mean ± SD: 24.92 ± 2.87 y; six women)".
  - Exposure: ~4 h of reading before bedtime on **5 consecutive evenings**, either on an LE-eBook ("iPad", "set to maximum brightness") or a printed book, in very dim room light.
  - Fixed bedtime 22:00–06:00.
  - Melatonin suppression and phase were each analysed in 11 participants.
- **Key results (full text):**
  - Melatonin: "suppressed evening levels of melatonin by 55.12 ± 20.12%, whereas the print-book condition showed no suppression (−18.77 ± 39.57%)".
  - Circadian timing: "Dim light melatonin onset was >1.5 h later … (22:31 ± 0:42) than in the print-book condition (21:01 ± 0:49; P < 0.001)".
  - Sleep latency: "nearly 10 min longer to fall asleep … (25.65 ± 18.78 min vs. 15.75 ± 13.09 min; P = 0.009)".
  - REM: "109.04 ± 26.25 min vs. 120.86 ± 25.32 min … P = 0.03". "There was no difference between conditions in TST, sleep efficiency, or the duration of non-REM sleep".
  - Evening: less sleepy an hour before bedtime (P < 0.01).
  - Morning: "feeling sleepier the morning after reading an LE-eBook … (P < 0.001)", and "it took them hours longer to fully 'wake up'".
  - Light at the eye: LE-eBook 31.73 photopic lux vs printed book 0.91.
- **Authors' limitations:**
  - The eReader was at maximum brightness vs very dim print.
  - Sessions were 4 h long.
  - There was no long-wavelength device control, so "our findings may be due to the difference in irradiance level rather than spectral composition".
- **Verdict: correct** (melatonin ↓, sleep onset later, next-morning alertness ↓). N = 12.

## 5. Ansfield, Wegner & Bowser (1996)

- **Citation:** Ansfield ME, Wegner DM, Bowser R. Ironic effects of sleep urgency. *Behaviour Research and Therapy*. 1996;34(7):523–531.
- **IDs:** PMID 8826759 · DOI 10.1016/0005-7967(96)00031-9
- **Read:** PubMed abstract only. The full text is paywalled (Unpaywall: closed), and Wegner's Harvard page returned 403.
- **Design (abstract):**
  - "Normal sleepers were instructed either to fall asleep as quickly as they could or to fall asleep whenever they desired".
  - Load conditions: "under a high mental load (listening to John Philip Sousa marches) or a low mental load (listening to sleep-conducive new age music)."
  - **N, setting and how sleep onset was measured are not in the abstract → unverifiable.**
- **Key results (abstract):**
  - "Under low load, participants trying to fall asleep quickly did so faster than those attempting only to fall asleep whenever they desired."
  - "Under high load, however, … sleep onset latency was greater for participants attempting to fall asleep quickly than for those not attempting to do so."
- **Verdict: correct on the core finding.** Two caveats:
  1. The abstract calls the Sousa marches a "high mental load", not "arousing music".
  2. With low load (calm music), trying to fall asleep fast actually **worked**, so the ironic effect appeared only under load. N cannot be confirmed.

## 6. Harvey (2002)

- **Citation:** Harvey AG. A cognitive model of insomnia. *Behaviour Research and Therapy*. 2002;40(8):869–893.
- **IDs:** PMID 12186352 · DOI 10.1016/s0005-7967(01)00061-4
- **Read:** PubMed abstract only (full text paywalled).
- **Design:** A theoretical model paper with a literature review. No new sample, no N.
- **Key content (abstract):**
  - Insomnia sufferers are "overly worried about their sleep and about the daytime consequences of not getting enough sleep". This "triggers both autonomic arousal and emotional distress".
  - "selective attention towards and monitoring of internal and external sleep-related threat cues".
  - Together these trick "the individual into overestimating the extent of the perceived deficit in sleep and daytime performance".
  - "Counterproductive safety behaviours (including thought control, imagery control, emotional inhibition, and difficulty problem solving) and erroneous beliefs about sleep and the benefits of worry are highlighted as exacerbating factors."
  - End point: anxiety "may culminate in a real deficit in sleep and daytime functioning".
- **Verdict: correct** (worry, selective attention to sleep-related threat, safety behaviours). It's a model, not an experiment.

## 7. Broomfield & Espie (2003)

- **Citation:** Broomfield NM, Espie CA. Initial insomnia and paradoxical intention: an experimental investigation of putative mechanisms using subjective and actigraphic measurement of sleep. *Behavioural and Cognitive Psychotherapy*. 2003;31(3):313–324.
- **IDs:** DOI 10.1017/S1352465803003060 · **no PMID** (not found in PubMed by author or title search).
- **Read:** Publisher abstract via Crossref. Full text paywalled.
- **Design / N:** "Following a seven-night baseline, 34 sleep-onset insomniacs were randomly allocated to 14 nights of PI, or to a control (no PI) condition." Sleep was measured subjectively and by actigraphy.
- **Key results (abstract):**
  - PI is "thought to operate by eliminating voluntary sleep effort, thereby ameliorating sleep performance anxiety".
  - Results: "participants allocated to PI, relative to controls, showed a significant reduction in sleep effort, and sleep performance anxiety."
  - "Sleep-onset latency (SOL) differences between PI participants and controls using an objective sleep measure were not observed, although an underlying trend for significantly lowered subjective SOL amongst PI participants was demonstrated" (wording as in the abstract).
- **Verdict: correct.** What changed: sleep effort ↓ and sleep performance anxiety ↓.
  - Objective (actigraphic) sleep latency did not differ.
  - Subjective sleep latency showed only a "trend".
  - Don't say PI "made people fall asleep faster" as a measured fact.

## 8. Baron, Abbott, Jao, Manalo & Mullen (2017)

- **Citation:** Baron KG, Abbott S, Jao N, Manalo N, Mullen R. Orthosomnia: Are Some Patients Taking the Quantified Self Too Far? *Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine*. 2017;13(2):351–354.
- **IDs:** PMID 27855740 · PMCID PMC5263088 · DOI 10.5664/jcsm.6472
- **Read:** Full text (PMC HTML). Article type: case report (case series).
- **Design / N:** "**Three cases** are presented in this case series":
  - Mr. R, 40-year-old man
  - Ms. B, 27-year-old woman
  - Mr. S, 69-year-old man
- **Key content:**
  - Term: "We termed this condition 'orthosomnia,' with 'ortho' meaning straight or correct, and 'somnia' meaning sleep, because patients are preoccupied or concerned with improving or perfecting their wearable sleep data". This is by analogy with orthorexia.
  - Abstract: tracker-based reasoning "may become a perfectionistic quest for the ideal sleep".
  - "all three patients were spending excessive time in bed in attempts to increase the sleep duration reported by the sleep tracker, which may have exacerbated their insomnia."
  - Ms. B's in-lab PSG showed increased deep (N3) sleep. She still asked, "Then why does my Fitbit say I am sleeping poorly?"
  - Limitation: "we do not know about the patients' sleep before the sleep tracker to know whether the tracker caused the sleep problem."
- **Verdict: correct.** 3 case reports. This is not a prevalence study.

## 9. Scullin, Krueger, Ballard, Pruett & Bliwise (2018)

- **Citation:** Scullin MK, Krueger ML, Ballard HK, Pruett N, Bliwise DL. The effects of bedtime writing on difficulty falling asleep: A polysomnographic study comparing to-do lists and completed activity lists. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*. 2018;147(1):139–146 (epub 23 Oct 2017).
- **IDs:** PMID 29058942 · PMCID PMC5758411 · DOI 10.1037/xge0000374
- **Read:** PubMed abstract + full text (PMC).
- **Design / N:**
  - "Fifty-seven healthy young adults (18-30)" (60 recruited, 3 excluded).
  - Randomly assigned to write for 5 min before overnight PSG on their first lab night: to-do list (n = 28) or completed-activities list (n = 29).
  - Lights out ≈ 22:30. There was no "no-writing" control group.
- **Key results:**
  - Table 1, PSG sleep-onset latency: **completed list 25.09 (SD 15.94) min vs to-do list 15.82 (SD 14.07) min**, t(55) = 2.32, p = 0.02, d = 0.63.
  - The difference (≈ 9.3 min) is **my arithmetic from Table 1**. The paper's text gives no "X minutes faster" figure.
  - Abstract: "The more specifically participants wrote their to-do list, the faster they subsequently fell asleep". For to-do lists, r(26) = −.39, p = .04.
  - Total sleep time did not differ.
- **Verdict: correct.** PSG, N = 57, ~9 min faster sleep onset (computed from the means).

## 10. Yoo, Gujar, Hu, Jolesz & Walker (2007)

- **Citation:** Yoo SS, Gujar N, Hu P, Jolesz FA, Walker MP. The human emotional brain without sleep — a prefrontal amygdala disconnect. *Current Biology*. 2007;17(20):R877–R878.
- **IDs:** PMID 17956744 · DOI 10.1016/j.cub.2007.08.007
- **Read:** PubMed has no abstract (it's a Correspondence). I read the full 2-page publisher PDF from the Walker lab reprint page (walkerlab.berkeley.edu/reprints/Yoo-Walker_CurrBiol_2007.pdf). cell.com and ScienceDirect blocked automated access.
- **Design / N:**
  - Between-subjects fMRI: "Twenty-six healthy participants age 18–30 years (mean 24.1, s.d. ± 2.3) were assigned to either a sleep-deprivation group (n = 14; 7 males) or sleep-control group (n = 12; 6 males)."
  - The deprived group was "awake across Day-1, Night-1 and Day-2, accumulating **approximately 35 hours** of total sleep deprivation before scanning (5PM, Day-2)".
  - Task: "100 images from a standardized picture set" (IAPS), ranging from neutral to increasingly aversive.
- **Key results:**
  - "those in the sleep-deprivation condition exhibited a remarkable **+60% greater magnitude of amygdala activation, relative to the control group** (averaged left and right amygdala, t(24) = 3.2, P = 0.004…)".
  - "a three-fold increase in the extent of amygdala volume that was activated" (t(24) = 2.8, P = 0.009).
  - Activation was similar for the most neutral pictures; the differences were for the most negative ones.
  - "significantly stronger connectivity between the amygdala and the MPFC in those that had slept". The deprived group instead had stronger connectivity with autonomic brainstem regions.
  - The authors call the implications "speculative".
- **Verdict: correct** (N = 26, ~35 h, "+60%").
  - Precision note: the 60% compares **two different groups** (deprived vs rested). It does not compare the same people before and after.
  - It refers to the magnitude of activation to the negative-picture gradient.

## 11. Ben Simon & Walker (2018)

- **Citation:** Ben Simon E, Walker MP. Sleep loss causes social withdrawal and loneliness. *Nature Communications*. 2018;9(1):3146.
- **IDs:** PMID 30108218 · PMCID PMC6092357 · DOI 10.1038/s41467-018-05377-0
- **Read:** PubMed abstract + full text (PMC). The abstract gives no Ns.
- **Design / N:**
  - In-lab study: **18** healthy adults, 18–24 y (nine women). Counterbalanced within-subject design: "one after a normal night of sleep, and one after 24 h of total sleep deprivation". Measures were a social-distance task (in person + fMRI version) and filmed interviews.
  - Online study 1 (MTurk): **138** participants (of 293 recruited), 2 nights of natural sleep with next-day loneliness.
  - Online study 2: **1,033** independent judges rated the filmed interviews, blind to condition.
- **Key results:**
  - Social distance after deprivation rose by "13.2% and 17.7%" (in-person and computerized tasks).
  - fMRI: activity in the "Near Space" network ↑ and the theory-of-mind network ↓.
  - Judges "rated sleep-deprived participants as being significantly lonelier".
  - Judges also "rated themselves as feeling significantly lonelier after having viewed a video of a participant when sleep-deprived". The lonelier a judge rated someone, the lonelier the judge felt (R = 0.66).
  - The desire to interact fell (−0.08 ± 0.03), and collaboration choice dropped 8%.
  - Night-to-night drops in sleep efficiency predicted next-day loneliness increases (online study 1).
  - The authors compare the distance increase to "approximately one-third of those reported in markedly asocial disorders, such as autism and schizophrenia".
- **Verdict: correct** (more social distance; others rated them lonelier and felt lonelier themselves). Ns: 18 / 138 / 1,033.

## 12. Gordon & Chen (2014)

- **Citation:** Gordon AM, Chen S. The role of sleep in interpersonal conflict: Do sleepless nights mean worse fights? *Social Psychological and Personality Science*. 2014;5(2):168–175 (online 14 May 2013).
- **IDs:** DOI 10.1177/1948550613488952 · **no PMID** (not in PubMed).
- **Read:** Publisher abstract (Crossref) + full text. The full text is the OnlineFirst version of record hosted on Amie Gordon's lab site (sites.lsa.umich.edu/whirl).
- **Design / N:**
  - **Study 1:** 14-day daily diary. "Seventy-eight (65 female) individuals in romantic relationships participated for course credit". Three were excluded; the remaining participants completed 908 diaries (mean 12.1 days). Individuals were studied, **not couples**.
  - **Study 2:** "71 heterosexual couples from the community" (two excluded). They had a lab conflict conversation ("5 min to work toward a resolution while videotaped").
  - In Study 2, sleep was **self-reported** with 3 items about the previous night. Sleep was not manipulated in either study, so both are correlational.
- **Key results:**
  - Study 1: "participants reported more conflict on days following a relatively poor night of sleep, B = .05, t(634) = 2.67, p < .01", controlling for the previous day's conflict.
  - Study 2: "The worse participants slept the previous night, the lower their empathic accuracy" (B = .09). Their partners "were less able to accurately assess their emotions" (B = .11).
  - Study 2: a lower positive-to-negative affect ratio, both self-reported and observed.
  - Abstract: "Conflict resolution occurred most when both partners were well rested." Full text: "having just one partner who slept poorly was enough to hinder conflict resolution."
  - Effects "were not explained by stress, anxiety, depression, lack of relationship satisfaction, or by partners being the source of poor sleep."
- **Verdict: partly.** The sleep → conflict link is confirmed. But "in couples" applies only to Study 2; Study 1 was individuals (mostly female students). Present it as an association, not an experiment.

## 13. Van Dongen, Maislin, Mullington & Dinges (2003)

- **Citation:** Van Dongen HPA, Maislin G, Mullington JM, Dinges DF. The cumulative cost of additional wakefulness: dose-response effects on neurobehavioral functions and sleep physiology from chronic sleep restriction and total sleep deprivation. *Sleep*. 2003;26(2):117–126.
- **IDs:** PMID 12683469 · DOI 10.1093/sleep/26.2.117. PubMed lists an erratum: *Sleep* 2004;27(4):600. **I did not read the erratum.**
- **Read:** PubMed abstract + the published PDF (a third-party copy whose footer says it was downloaded from academic.oup.com). The UPenn-hosted copy could not be fetched because its TLS chain wasn't trusted here.
- **Design / N:**
  - "A total of n = 48 healthy adults (ages 21-38)".
  - Chronic restriction: 4 h, 6 h or 8 h time in bed for 14 days. Groups: 8 h n = 9; 6 h n = 13; 4 h n = 13.
  - Total sleep deprivation: 3 nights (88 h awake), n = 13.
- **Key results:**
  - Abstract: 4 h or 6 h for 14 days "resulted in significant cumulative, dose-dependent deficits in cognitive performance on all tasks".
  - Full text, per condition:
    - "Subjects in the **6 h** sleep period condition also reached levels of impairment equivalent to those observed after **1 night** of total sleep loss for lapses in behavioral alertness and working memory performance".
    - "In the **4 h** sleep period condition, lapses in behavioral alertness and reductions in working memory performance reached levels equivalent to those observed after **2 nights** without sleep". The 4 h group's cognitive throughput was ≈ 1 night.
  - Abstract summary: "6 h or less per night produced cognitive performance deficits equivalent to up to 2 nights of total sleep deprivation".
  - Sleepiness (abstract): "Subjective sleepiness ratings showed an acute response to sleep restriction but only small further increases on subsequent days, and did not significantly differentiate the 6 h and 4 h conditions".
  - Full text: ~1 unit on the Stanford Sleepiness Scale after 14 days of 6 h or 4 h, vs a response that "spanned more than 2 units" after 3 days of total deprivation. "Sleepiness ratings suggest that subjects were largely unaware of these increasing cognitive deficits".
  - The 8 h group showed only minor, non-significant changes.
- **Verdict: partly.** Correction: **6 h/night × 14 days ≈ 1 night** of total deprivation (attention lapses, working memory). "Up to 2 nights" is the **4 h** group. The "sleepiness rose only slightly" part is correct.

## 14. Depner et al. (2019)

- **Citation:** Depner CM, Melanson EL, Eckel RH, Snell-Bergeon JK, Perreault L, Bergman BC, Higgins JA, Guerin MK, Stothard ER, Morton SJ, Wright KP Jr. Ad libitum Weekend Recovery Sleep Fails to Prevent Metabolic Dysregulation during a Repeating Pattern of Insufficient Sleep and Weekend Recovery Sleep. *Current Biology*. 2019;29(6):957–967.e4 (epub 28 Feb 2019).
- **IDs:** PMID 30827911 · PMCID PMC12798825 · DOI 10.1016/j.cub.2019.01.069
- **Read:** PubMed abstract + full text (PMC author manuscript).
- **Design / N:**
  - In-laboratory randomized study at a clinical research center. "Thirty-six healthy participants (18 women/18 men) aged 25.5 ± 4.7y".
  - Arms:
    - CON: 9 h sleep opportunities, n = 8.
    - SR: 5 h, no weekend recovery, n = 14.
    - WR: 5-day workweek of 5 h, then ad libitum weekend (≥10 h in bed, naps allowed), then 2 nights of 5 h, n = 14.
  - Food was ad libitum.
- **Key results (abstract unless noted):**
  - "insufficient sleep increased after-dinner energy intake and body weight versus baseline" (SR and WR).
  - Weekend: "participants cumulatively slept ∼1.1 h more than baseline". Full text: ~3.0 h extra over Friday and Saturday nights, but less sleep on Sunday night. The weekend did not repay "the more than 12h of sleep lost during the workweek".
  - After the weekend: "the circadian phase was delayed, and after-dinner energy intake and body weight increased versus baseline."
  - Insulin sensitivity: "In SR, whole-body insulin sensitivity decreased ∼13% …; in WR, whole-body, hepatic, and muscle insulin sensitivity decreased ∼9%-27%".
    - Full text: WR whole-body ~27%, muscle ~9%, hepatic ~23%.
    - The hepatic drop was non-significant in the full model, P = 0.054.
  - Sex difference: on the weekend women slept less than men, and energy intake fell to baseline in women but not men.
  - Conclusion: "weekend recovery sleep is not an effective strategy to prevent metabolic dysregulation associated with recurrent insufficient sleep."
- **Verdict:** your line only asked for N, design and results; all are confirmed above.

## 15. Wittmann, Dinich, Merrow & Roenneberg (2006)

- **Citation:** Wittmann M, Dinich J, Merrow M, Roenneberg T. Social jetlag: misalignment of biological and social time. *Chronobiology International*. 2006;23(1-2):497–509.
- **IDs:** PMID 16687322 · DOI 10.1080/07420520500545979
- **Read:** PubMed abstract only (full text paywalled; no open copy found).
- **Design / N:** Questionnaire study. "A total of 501 volunteers filled out the Munich ChronoType Questionnaire (MCTQ)", plus sleep quality (SF-A), wellbeing (Basler Befindlichkeitsbogen, POMS) and stimulant use.
- **Definition (abstract):**
  - "Late chronotypes show the largest differences in sleep timing between work and free days leading to a considerable sleep debt on work days, for which they compensate on free days."
  - "The discrepancy between work and free days, between social and biological time, can be described as 'social jetlag.'"
- **Findings (abstract):**
  - "Associations of chronotype, wellbeing, and stimulant consumption are strongest in teenagers and young adults up to age 25 yrs."
  - "The most striking correlation exists between chronotype and smoking, which is significantly higher in late chronotypes of all ages (except for those in retirement)."
  - These correlations "are most probably a consequence of social jetlag … rather than a simple association to different chronotypes".
  - Recommendation: "work (and school) schedules should be adapted to chronotype whenever possible."
- **Verdict: correct for the conceptual definition and headline findings.** Unverifiable from this paper:
  - The exact operational formula. Secondary sources give "|mid-sleep on free days − mid-sleep on workdays|", but that is **not in the abstract** and I couldn't read the full text.
  - Any effect sizes.

## 16. Golder & Macy (2011)

- **Citation:** Golder SA, Macy MW. Diurnal and seasonal mood vary with work, sleep, and daylength across diverse cultures. *Science*. 2011;333(6051):1878–1881.
- **IDs:** PMID 21960633 · DOI 10.1126/science.1202775
- **Read:** PubMed abstract + full text. The full text is a copy of the Science PDF on an Indiana University course-readings page; science.org blocked automated access.
- **Design / N:**
  - "about 2.4 million individuals from across the globe and 509 million messages authored between February 2008 and January 2010".
  - Users with <25 messages were excluded, and up to 400 messages were collected per user.
  - 84 countries; "all reported results include only English speakers".
  - Positive and negative affect were scored with the LIWC lexicon and mean-centred within each individual.
- **Key results (full text):**
  - "**PA had two peaks: relatively early in the morning and again near midnight.**" PA "decreased midmorning (at the start of the work day) and increased in the evening".
  - "**NA was lowest in the morning and rose throughout the day to a nighttime peak**".
  - PA was higher on Saturday and Sunday (M = 0.058) than on weekdays (M = 0.054).
  - Weekend morning peak "delayed by nearly 2 hours (M = 9:48 a.m. versus M = 7:55 a.m., P < 0.001)". The UAE (Fri–Sat weekend) showed the same shift (9:53 vs 8:04 a.m.).
  - Seasonal: there was no effect of *absolute* daylength. Baseline PA was higher when days were lengthening, highest near the spring equinox. NA did not change.
  - Limitation: "lexical analysis measures the expression of affect, not the experience."
- **Verdict:** confirmed (see the peak timings above).

## 17. Carney & Waters (2006)

- **Citation:** Carney CE, Waters WF. Effects of a structured problem-solving procedure on pre-sleep cognitive arousal in college students with insomnia. *Behavioral Sleep Medicine*. 2006;4(1):13–28.
- **IDs:** PMID 16390282 · DOI 10.1207/s15402010bsm0401_2
- **Read:** PubMed abstract (article full text paywalled).
  - Also: the abstract of Carney's 2003 LSU doctoral dissertation, "The effects of an **evening** structured problem-solving procedure in undergraduate college students with insomnia". It appears to be the same study (same N = 33 and design).
  - The dissertation is a separate document from the article, and its PDF download was blocked (403).
- **Design / N (article abstract):**
  - A "controlled group design" in "an undergraduate population reporting insomnia".
  - "After 2 baseline nights, participants (N = 33) either recorded possible solutions to worries (constructive worry group) or recorded worries and completed worry questionnaires (worry group) for 5 nights."
  - The procedure is described as "similar to Espie and Lindsay's (1987) 'worry control' procedure".
- **Key results:**
  - Article: "the constructive worry group had decreased pre-sleep cognitive arousal relative to the worry group and relative to baseline scores."
  - Dissertation abstract: "there were no significant effects on somatic arousal, anxiety, Sleep Diary or actigraphy sleep variables."
- **Verdict: correct** on the method (constructive worry / structured problem-solving), the outcome (pre-sleep cognitive arousal ↓) and N = 33. Caveats:
  - **"Before bed" is unverifiable.** The article abstract gives no timing; the dissertation title says "evening".
  - Per the dissertation abstract, sleep itself (diary/actigraphy) did **not** improve significantly. Don't claim it helps people fall asleep faster.

## 18. Axelsson et al. (2010)

- **Citation:** Axelsson J, Sundelin T, Ingre M, Van Someren EJW, Olsson A, Lekander M. Beauty sleep: experimental study on the perceived health and attractiveness of sleep deprived people. *BMJ*. 2010;341:c6614.
- **IDs:** PMID 21156746 · PMCID PMC3001961 · DOI 10.1136/bmj.c6614
- **Read:** PubMed abstract + full text (PMC).
- **Design / N:**
  - "23 healthy, sleep deprived adults (age 18-31) who were photographed and 65 untrained observers (age 18-61) who rated the photographs". Full text: 24 enrolled; 11 women in the final 23.
  - Within-subject, balanced design. Photos were taken 14:00–15:00 after normal sleep (≥8 h) vs after "sleep deprivation (31 hours of wakefulness after a night of reduced sleep)". The reduced night was sleep 02:00–07:00 (≈5 h).
  - Observers saw each photo for six seconds and rated it on a 100 mm visual analogue scale.
- **Key results (abstract):** sleep-deprived faces were rated
  - "less healthy (… mean 63 (SE 2) v 68 (SE 2), P<0.001)"
  - "more tired (53 (SE 3) v 44 (SE 3), P<0.001)"
  - "less attractive (38 (SE 2) v 40 (SE 2), P<0.001)"
  - Full text: the effects on health and attractiveness were "significantly mediated by tiredness". A few people were rated *better* when sleep-deprived.
- **Verdict: correct.** N = 23 photographed, 65 raters. The absolute differences are small: 2 mm on a 100 mm scale for attractiveness.

## 19. Tubbs, Fernandez, Grandner, Perlis & Klerman (2022)

- **Citation:** Tubbs AS, Fernandez FX, Grandner MA, Perlis ML, Klerman EB. The Mind After Midnight: Nocturnal Wakefulness, Behavioral Dysregulation, and Psychopathology. *Frontiers in Network Physiology*. 2022;1:830338 (epub 3 Mar 2022; the DOI carries 2021).
- **IDs:** PMID 35538929 · PMCID PMC9083440 · DOI 10.3389/fnetp.2021.830338
- **Read:** PubMed abstract + full text (PMC). Article type in PMC: "review-article". The journal labels it a **"Mini Review"**.
- **Nature of paper:** **A review proposing a hypothesis, with no new empirical data.**
  - Abstract: "This review summarizes the evidence for day-night alterations in maladaptive behaviors, including suicide, violent crime, and substance use … Based on this evidence, we propose the Mind after Midnight hypothesis in which attentional biases, negative affect, altered reward processing, and prefrontal disinhibition interact to promote behavioral dysregulation and psychiatric disorders."
- **Numbers it cites:** these come from other studies and I did **not** verify those sources.
  - Perlis et al. 2016: suicide risk "three-fold greater between midnight and 6 AM" after adjusting for population wakefulness.
  - Figure 1, from ATUS and NVDRS data for 2003–2010: "4.25-fold greater risk at 2AM than the 24-h average".
  - Veterans are "eight-fold more likely to die by suicide between midnight and 3 AM" (McCarthy et al. 2019).
  - The heroin-user and college-student scenarios in the text are hypothetical illustrations.
- **Verdict:** it's a hypothesis/review paper. If you quote any night-time risk figure, cite and check the original study, not this review.
