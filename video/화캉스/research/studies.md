## 1. Albulescu et al. (2022), micro-break meta-analysis

- **Citation:** Albulescu, P., Macsinga, I., Rusu, A., Sulea, C., Bodnaru, A., & Tulbure, B. T. (2022). "Give me a break!" A systematic review and meta-analysis on the efficacy of micro-breaks for increasing well-being and performance. *PLoS ONE, 17*(8), e0272460.
- **IDs:** DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0272460 · PMID 36044424 · PMCID PMC9432722
- **Read:** full text (Europe PMC XML).
- **Design / N:** a meta-analysis of experimental and quasi-experimental between-group studies. "The systematic search revealed 19 records, which resulted in 22 independent study samples (N = 2335)."
  - Sample mix (full text): "Eleven studies used samples of students, whereas the other ten studies tested their hypotheses on samples of employees." One used "normal volunteers". Mean age 31.2.
  - Settings: "Most of the studies took place in a laboratory (n = 13); a smaller number was in an organizational/workplace setting (n = 9)." Randomised n = 15, non-random n = 7.
- **Definition of a micro-break:** "short discontinuities in one's tasks of no longer than 10 minutes". Breaks actually tested: "ranging between 8 seconds and 10 minutes."
- **Key results (abstract):**
  - "statistically significant but small effects of micro-breaks in boosting vigor (d = .36, p < .001; k = 9, n = 913), reducing fatigue (d = .35, p < .001; k = 9, n = 803), and a non-significant effect on increasing overall performance (d = .16, p = .116; k = 15, n = 1132)."
  - "Sub-groups analyses on performance types revealed significant effects only for tasks with less cognitive demands."
  - "A meta-regression showed that the longer the break, the greater the boost was on performance." The full text gives "(b = .07, p = .006, R2 = .34)".
  - "recovering from highly depleting tasks may need more than 10-minute breaks"
  - By task type (full text): cognitive "d = -.09, 95%CI [-.39, .30], p = .541" (not significant); creative "d = .38 … p = .006"; clerical "d = .56 … p = .047". The clerical result "is based only on two studies".
  - Discussion: "no study found significantly decreased performance when a break was introduced between tasks". The authors also write that "taking a break at least does not harm it."
  - Vigor and fatigue: "none of the considered moderators impacted the efficacy of micro-breaks (all ps > .050)".
- **Verdict:** **Correct.** Vigor goes up and fatigue goes down, with small effects. The performance effect was non-significant overall; it was significant only for less cognitively demanding tasks, and grew with break length. Precise numbers: 19 records, 22 samples, N = 2335, breaks ≤10 min. Caveat: about half the samples were students and most studies were lab-based.

## 2. Kim, Park & Niu (2017), micro-break activities

- **Citation:** Kim, S., Park, Y., & Niu, Q. (2017). Micro-break activities at work to recover from daily work demands. *Journal of Organizational Behavior, 38*(1), 28–44. Published online 2 May 2016 according to Crossref.
- **IDs:** DOI 10.1002/job.2109 (not in PubMed)
- **Read:** the official abstract as deposited with Crossref by Wiley. The full text is paywalled and has no open copy (Unpaywall/OpenAlex).
- **Design / N:** a daily diary study. "For 10 consecutive workdays, 86 South Korean office workers (842 data points) reported their specific daily work demands right after their lunch hour (Time 1) and then reported their engagement in micro‐break activities during the afternoon and negative affective state at the end of the workday (Time 2)."
- **Activities:** "four categories of micro‐break activities—relaxation, nutrition‐intake, social, and cognitive activities"
- **Key results:**
  - "As expected, relaxation and social activities reduced the effects of work demands on end‐of‐workday negative affect."
  - "Nutrition intake of beverages and snacks did not have a significant moderating effect."
  - "Post hoc analyses, however, revealed that only caffeinated beverages reduced work demands effects on negative affect."
  - "Unexpectedly, cognitive activities aggravated the effects of work demands on negative affect."
- **Verdict:** **Correct, with nuance.** The activities buffer (moderate) the link between work demands and end-of-day negative affect; the abstract does not report simple main effects. Cognitive micro-breaks made the link *stronger*. The sample is Korean office workers, which fits the video well.
- **UNVERIFIED:** which concrete behaviours counted in each category (for example, whether phone or internet use counted as "cognitive"). The abstract doesn't list the items.

## 3. Lim (2002), cyberloafing and justice

- **Citation:** Lim, V. K. G. (2002). The IT way of loafing on the job: cyberloafing, neutralizing and organizational justice. *Journal of Organizational Behavior, 23*(5), 675–694.
- **IDs:** DOI 10.1002/job.161
- **Read:** official abstract (Crossref/Wiley). The full text is paywalled.
- **Design / N:** "Data were collected with the use of an electronic questionnaire and focus group interviews from 188 working adults with access to the internet at the workplace. Results of structural equation modelling provided empirical support for all of our hypotheses."
- **Key content:**
  - Definition: "cyberloafing—the act of employees using their companies' internet access for personal purposes during work hours. Cyberloafing, thus, represents a form of production deviance."
  - Model: "when individuals perceived their organizations to be distributively, procedurally and interactionally unjust, they were likely to invoke the metaphor of the ledger as a neutralization technique to legitimize their subsequent engagement in the act of cyberloafing."
- **Verdict:** **Correct.** The specific neutralization technique is the "metaphor of the ledger" (roughly, "the company owes me"). Caveats: the data are cross-sectional and self-reported, and this is 2002 desktop internet use on company access, not smartphones.

## 5. Trougakos, Beal, Green & Weiss (2008), "Making the break count"

- **Citation:** Trougakos, J. P., Beal, D. J., Green, S. G., & Weiss, H. M. (2008). Making the break count: An episodic examination of recovery activities, emotional experiences, and positive affective displays. *Academy of Management Journal, 51*(1), 131–146.
- **IDs:** DOI 10.5465/amj.2008.30764063
- **Read:** the abstract as indexed by OpenAlex. The AOM page returned 403, and Semantic Scholar notes that the publisher withholds the abstract. The full text is paywalled.
- **Design / N:** "In results based on data collected from 64 cheerleading instructors via experience sampling, break activities are related to emotional experiences and positive affective displays."
- **Key result:** "Moreover, supporting regulatory resource theory, break activities' impact on positive affective displays goes beyond the effects of emotional experiences."
- **On "respite vs chores":**
  - These labels are not in the 2008 abstract. They appear explicitly in a later chapter: Trougakos, J. P., & Hideg, I. (2009). Momentary work recovery: The role of within-day work breaks. In *Current Perspectives on Job-Stress Recovery* (Research in Occupational Stress and Well Being, Vol. 7). Emerald. DOI 10.1108/S1479-3555(2009)0000007005. Its abstract says: "we distinguish between two types of breaks, respites and chores".
  - Secondary description of the 2008 study, in de Bloom, Kinnunen & Korpela (2014), *BMC Public Health* 14:488 (PMID 24885178): "Restful and enjoyable activities (called respites) provided greater recovery than more effortful and not preferred activities (called chores)."
- **Verdict:** **Partly verified.** The ESM design, the 64 cheerleading instructors and the activity–emotion–display link are confirmed. **UNVERIFIED** from the primary text: the specific categories (relaxing, socializing, chores), which emotions changed, and effect sizes. If you say "respites vs chores", attribute the framework to Trougakos & Hideg (2009).

## 6. Hunter & Wu (2016), "Give me a better break"

- **Citation:** Hunter, E. M., & Wu, C. (2016). Give me a better break: Choosing workday break activities to maximize resource recovery. *Journal of Applied Psychology, 101*(2), 302–311. Epub 10 Aug 2015.
- **IDs:** DOI 10.1037/apl0000045 · PMID 26375961
- **Read:** abstract (PubMed).
- **Design / N:** "using experience sampling methodology, we examined the characteristics of employee workday breaks with 95 employees across 5 workdays."
- **Key results:**
  - "Multilevel analysis results indicated that activities that were preferred and earlier in the work shift related to more resource recovery following the break."
  - "resources mediated the influence of preferred break activities and time of break on health symptoms and that resource recovery benefited person-level outcomes of emotional exhaustion, job satisfaction, and organizational citizenship behavior."
  - "break length interacted with the number of breaks per day such that longer breaks and frequent short breaks were associated with more resources than infrequent short breaks."
- **Verdict:** **Correct.** N = 95 employees over 5 workdays. The wording is "earlier in the work shift", not literally "morning". The number of break reports is UNVERIFIED (not in the abstract).

## 7. Bernstein & Turban (2018), open offices

- **Citation:** Bernstein, E. S., & Turban, S. (2018). The impact of the 'open' workspace on human collaboration. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 373*(1753), 20170239.
- **IDs:** DOI 10.1098/rstb.2017.0239 · PMID 29967303 · PMCID PMC6030579
- **Read:** full text (Europe PMC).
- **Design:** two intervention-based field studies at corporate headquarters moving to open offices. Face-to-face (F2F) interaction was measured with sociometric badges; email and IM came from company servers.
- **Study 1 (N = 52):** "a cluster of 52 (roughly 40%) agreed to participate". Data covered 15 workdays before the move and 15 workdays about three months later.
  - "the 52 participants now spent 72% less time interacting F2F"
  - "participants collectively sent 56% (66) more emails to other participants over 15 days, received 20% (78) more emails from other participants, and were cc'd on 41% (27) more emails"
  - "IM message activity increased by 67% (99 more messages) and words sent by IM increased by 75% (850 more words)."
- **Study 2 (N = 100):** "100 employees from a single floor, which was roughly 45% of the employees on that floor". Data covered eight weeks before and eight weeks after.
  - "spent between 67% (Model 1, 12.79/17.99) and 71% (Model 2, 9.81/14.63) less time interacting F2F. Instead, they emailed each other between 22% (Model 3, 1.24/5.75) and 50% (Model 4, 1.54/3.07) more."
- **Summary sentence:** "open, unbounded offices reduce F2F interaction with a magnitude, in these contexts, of about 70%. Electronic interaction takes up at least some of the slack, increasing by roughly 20% to 50% (as measured by 'To:' received email)."
- **Useful for the video:** "an open expanse of proximal employees choosing to isolate themselves as best they can (e.g. by wearing large headphones [ref]) while appearing to be as busy as possible (since everyone can see them)."
- **Verdict:** **Correct** (about 70%, with an increase in electronic interaction). The evidence comes from only two companies.

## 9. Sicherman, Loewenstein, Seppi & Utkus (2016), "Financial attention"

- **Citation:** Sicherman, N., Loewenstein, G., Seppi, D. J., & Utkus, S. P. (2016). Financial attention. *Review of Financial Studies, 29*(4), 863–897. Published online 19 Nov 2015.
- **IDs:** DOI 10.1093/rfs/hhv073
- **Read:** the abstract on the OUP page (via WebFetch; OpenAlex has the identical text). The full text is paywalled. I also read the CMU press release (phys.org, 17 Dec 2015), which is secondary.
- **Abstract:**
  - "This paper investigates financial attention using novel panel data on daily investor online account logins."
  - "We find support for selective attention to portfolio information."
  - "Account logins fall by 9.5% after market declines."
  - "Investors also pay less attention when the VIX volatility index is high."
  - "The level of attention and the attention/return correlation are strongly related to investor demographics (gender, age) and financial position (wealth, holdings)."
- **Press release only (secondary):**
  - "over 852 million observations on day-to-day logins and trades for 1.1 million investors over two years"
  - The data came via "Stephen Utkus at Vanguard".
  - "account logins fell by 9.5 percent after a decline in the previous day's stock market."
  - The exact years of the data are UNVERIFIED.
- **Verdict:** **Correct.** Logins track market movements: there is less checking after declines (−9.5%) and when the VIX is high. These are US retirement-account investors, not Koreans.

## 10. Rao, Welcher, Zimmerman & Stumbo (1998), "Is coffee a colonic stimulant?"

- **Citation:** Rao, S. S., Welcher, K., Zimmerman, B., & Stumbo, P. (1998). Is coffee a colonic stimulant? *European Journal of Gastroenterology & Hepatology, 10*(2), 113–118.
- **IDs:** DOI 10.1097/00042737-199802000-00003 · PMID 9581985
- **Read:** abstract (PubMed). The full text is paywalled.
- **Design / N:** "ambulatory colonic manometry by placing a six-sensor solid-state probe up to the mid-transverse colon in 12 healthy subjects". Then: "over a 10 h period, subjects received four stimuli: 240 ml of three drinks at 45 degrees C in random order: black Colombian coffee (150 mg caffeine), decaffeinated coffee or water and 1000 kcal meal."
- **Key results:**
  - "Caffeinated coffee, decaffeinated coffee and meal induced more activity in the colon with a greater area under the curve of pressure waves (P < 0.01) and a greater number of propagated contractions (P < 0.05) when compared with water."
  - "The effects of decaffeinated coffee on colonic motility were not significantly different from those of water or caffeinated coffee and were lower (P < 0.05) than that of a meal."
  - Conclusion: "Caffeinated coffee stimulates colonic motor activity. Its magnitude is similar to a meal, 60% stronger than water and 23% stronger than decaffeinated coffee."
  - Note: the abstract is internally inconsistent about decaf vs water. The first results sentence groups decaf with the stimulants; a later one says decaf was not significantly different from water. Avoid claims about decaf.
- **The "share who feel an urge" is not in Rao 1998.** It comes from Brown, S. R., Cann, P. A., & Read, N. W. (1990). Effect of coffee on distal colon function. *Gut, 31*(4), 450–453. DOI 10.1136/gut.31.4.450 · PMID 2338272 · PMCID PMC1378422. I read the abstract (PubMed); the PDF was blocked.
  - "Ninety nine healthy young volunteers (58 men, 34 women, aged 17-27 years) answered a questionnaire concerning their bowel habit with particular reference to the effects of beverages. Twenty nine per cent (63% women) claimed that coffee induced a desire to defecate."
  - Note: 58 + 34 = 92, not 99, as printed in the abstract.
  - Manometry was done in "14 healthy-subjects (12 men, two women, eight of whom claimed coffee caused a desire to defecate (responders)". Result: "an increase in motility index within four minutes after ingestion of both regular and decaffeinated coffee (p less than 0.05) in the eight responders, but not in the six non-responders."
  - The effect "lasted at least 30 minutes". "There was no increase in the motility index in any subject after a drink of hot water."
- **Verdict:** **Partly.** The comparison of coffee with water, decaf and a meal is correct for Rao (n = 12). The urge percentage must be credited to Brown et al. 1990: 29% of 99 young UK volunteers.
- **Extra (supports the "morning bowel" idea):**
  - Narducci, F., Bassotti, G., Gaburri, M., & Morelli, A. (1987). *Gut, 28*(1), 17–25. PMID 3817580. Design: 24-hour colonic recordings in 14 healthy volunteers.
    - "Colonic motor activity was low before meals and minimal during sleep; the motility index increased significantly after meals and at morning awakening."
    - High-amplitude propagated contractions: "Most of these contractions occurred after morning awakening". They "were often felt as an urge to defecate or preceded defecation".
  - Rao, S. S., Sadeghi, P., Beaty, J., Kavlock, R., & Ackerson, K. (2001). Ambulatory 24-h colonic manometry in healthy humans. *Am J Physiol Gastrointest Liver Physiol, 280*(4), G629–G639. PMID 11254489. Design: 25 adults.
    - "Waking induced a threefold increase in motility, whereas meals induced a twofold increase."
    - High-amplitude waves occurred "mostly after waking, after meals, or with defecation."

## 11. Smartphone use on the toilet and hemorrhoids (2025)

- **Citation:** Ramprasad, C., Wu, C., Chang, J., Rangan, V., Iturrino, J., Ballou, S., Singh, P., Lembo, A., Nee, J., & Pasricha, T. (2025). Smartphone use on the toilet and the risk of hemorrhoids. *PLOS ONE, 20*(9), e0329983.
- **IDs:** DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0329983 · PMID 40901789 · PMCID PMC12407481
- **Read:** full text (Europe PMC), including Table 2 and the Figure 4 image (the forest plot of adjusted ORs).
- **Design / N:** a cross-sectional survey of adults having a screening colonoscopy at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, recruited "01/08/2024–15/12/2024" (as written). Hemorrhoids were taken from the endoscopy reports, plus a sensitivity check by two endoscopists.
  - "Of 143 consecutive participants who were invited, a total of 125 participants completed the survey"
  - "83 (66%) used smartphones while on the toilet."
- **Time on the toilet:** "37.3% of smartphone users spending more than five minutes per visit on the toilet, compared to 7.1% of non-smartphone users (p = 0.006)".

  | Time on toilet (Table 2) | Smartphone users (n = 83) | Non-users (n = 42) |
  |---|---|---|
  | <1 min | 8.4% | 19.0% |
  | 2–5 min | 54.2% | 73.8% |
  | 6–10 min | 24.1% | 7.1% |
  | 11–15 min | 9.6% | 0% |
  | >15 min | 3.6% | 0% |

- **Main result:** "43% of all respondents had hemorrhoids visualized on colonoscopy." The text says smartphone use "was associated with a 46% increased risk of hemorrhoids (p = 0.044) after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, exercise activity, straining, and fiber intake".
  - Figure 4: **adjusted OR 1.46 (95% CI 1.01–2.14)**. This is an odds ratio, despite the word "risk", and the CI's lower bound is close to 1. Straining had aOR 1.10 (0.84–1.46), not significant.
- **What people did on the phone:** "reading "news" (54.3%), followed by "social media" (44.4%)".
- **Other details:** smartphone users were younger (mean age 55.4 vs 62.1). "only 35% of these users acknowledged that their smartphone use resulted in more time spent on the toilet at least 1–2 times per week or more".
- **Limitations the authors state:** cross-sectional design; self-reported time; the sample is screening-colonoscopy patients "age 45 and older".
- **Authors' advice:** "restrict smartphone use while on the toilet to under 5 minutes".
- **Contrary evidence:** Yazkan, C., Şahin, S., Yavuz, B., Mammadov, A., Özcan, Ö., & Dere, Ö. (2025). Toilet behaviors and lifestyle factors in anorectal diseases: a cross-sectional analysis. *Frontiers in Surgery, 12*, 1683286. PMID 41340992. Design: Turkey, 211 proctology patients vs 369 healthy controls.
  - Patients showed "prolonged toilet sitting (>20 min: 8.1% vs. 0.5%; p < 0.001), whereas smartphone use during defecation was lower (26.1% vs. 37.4%; p = 0.001)."
- **Verdict:** **Verified.** Present it as "associated with higher odds (1.46×) in one small US study". Mention that another 2025 study did not find more smartphone use among patients.

## 12. Sonnentag & Fritz (2007), Recovery Experience Questionnaire

- **Citation:** Sonnentag, S., & Fritz, C. (2007). The Recovery Experience Questionnaire: Development and validation of a measure for assessing recuperation and unwinding from work. *Journal of Occupational Health Psychology, 12*(3), 204–221.
- **IDs:** DOI 10.1037/1076-8998.12.3.204 · PMID 17638488
- **Read:** abstract (PubMed).
- **Design / N:**
  - "four self-report measures for assessing how individuals unwind and recuperate from work during leisure time were developed (Study 1)."
  - "Confirmatory factor analyses with a calibration and a cross-validation sample (total N=930) showed that four recovery experiences can be differentiated: psychological detachment from work, relaxation, mastery, and control (Study 2)."
  - "Examination of the nomological net in a subsample of Study 2 (N=271) revealed moderate relations of the recovery experiences with measures of job stressors and psychological well-being".
  - The scales are "short 4-item measures".
- **Verdict:** **Correct** (the four experiences). Caveat: the REQ was built for off-job leisure time. Applying it to a 5-minute toilet break at work is your extrapolation, not the paper's claim.

## 13. Goffman (1959), front stage / back stage

- **Citation:** Goffman, E. (1959). *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life.* Garden City, NY: Doubleday Anchor. An earlier version is dated 1956 (University of Edinburgh) and is cited as "Goffman 1956".
- **What I could read:** no legitimate full text. Internet Archive copies are lending-only and I did not use unofficial copies. I verified through two routes:
  - (a) Open Library's public "search inside" snippets, which come straight from four scanned copies of the Doubleday Anchor edition. Three are catalogued as 1959 and one as a 1990 printing; all four gave the same text.
  - (b) Published works that quote the book.
- **Back region definition:** quoted in Vainikka, J. T., & Saastamoinen, U. (2026), *Ambio* (DOI 10.1007/s13280-025-02332-4; PMCID PMC13437873; full text read). The back region is a "place, relative to a given performance, where the impression fostered by the performance is knowingly contradicted as a matter of course. […] Here the performer can relax; [s]he can drop his front forgo speaking his lines, and step out of character." (Goffman 1956, 69). Several books in the Open Library index cite the same definition as Goffman 1959: 112 (one says p. 114; one says "110-14").
- **Bathrooms:** these snippets come from the Doubleday Anchor edition scans:
  - "As suggested, the bathroom and bedroom, in all but lower-class homes, are places from which …" (the snippet is truncated; I could not see the end of the sentence).
  - "… there is done to food what in the bathroom and bedroom is done to the human body."
  - A secondary book (*The Survivor*; its Open Library identifier points to Terrence Des Pres) quotes the second sentence with the page reference "(123)".
- **Verdict:** **Concept correct.** Goffman does name the bathroom (together with the bedroom) as a back region, but **in the home**, as a place where bodies are prepared out of view. I could not check whether he discusses workplace toilets elsewhere in the book. Applying "backstage" to public bathrooms comes from later sociology (Cahill et al. 1985, item 14). Safe line for the video: Goffman counted the bathroom among the back regions of everyday life.

## 14. Peer-reviewed work on bathrooms at work: refuge and access

- **(a) Cahill, S. E., Distler, W., Lachowetz, C., Meaney, A., Tarallo, R., & Willard, T. (1985).** Meanwhile backstage: Public bathrooms and the interaction order. *Urban Life, 14*(1), 33–58. DOI 10.1177/0098303985014001002.
  - Read: abstract (Crossref).
  - "This article, based on observational research, provides a microsociological analysis of the behavior that routinely occurs in public bathrooms. Our findings indicate that occupants of public bathrooms routinely perform a number of interpersonal rituals and engage in a variety of backstage behaviors."
  - Sites and N: UNVERIFIED. This is the classic Goffman-to-bathroom link, but it covers *public* bathrooms, not specifically workplaces.
- **(b) Gunnarsson, M., & Törrönen, J. (2022).** Performing normality in working life among heavy substance users. *Nordic Studies on Alcohol and Drugs, 39*(5), 473–486. DOI 10.1177/14550725221108796 · PMID 36284744 · PMCID PMC9549223.
  - Read: full text. Design: "in-depth unstructured life story interviews, which were conducted over phone with 13 people"; Goffman framework.
  - "her performance of normality required different kinds of backstages: e.g. the bathroom, seeking a place where she could be alone".
  - This is peer-reviewed and shows a workplace bathroom used as a backstage, but in a drug-use context, so it fits a light video poorly.
- **(c) Restricted toilet access / toilet-break policies:**
